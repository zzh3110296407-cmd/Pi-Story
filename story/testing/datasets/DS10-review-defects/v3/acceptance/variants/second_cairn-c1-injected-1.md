# Two Marks in the Rain

Rain made the route board look newly painted. The red closure line was older than the rain, however, and Rafi could see where somebody had traced it twice to prevent travellers calling the first stroke ambiguous. Beyond the shelter, the lower path ran beneath a wall of water spilling from the hillside. He had guided people through worse weather on other routes. That experience gave him no right to pretend this route was open. He put his pack under the roof and waited for Jun to finish reading the board herself.

The route board confirmed that the lower path was closed for the night. Rafi read the closure board and knew the lower path was closed for the night. Jun asked whether closed meant impassable or forbidden. He said it meant they would not test the difference in darkness. Eamon, their porter, set down the food sack and began looking for a place where water would not drip into the fastening. They had enough daylight to make a camp and not enough to turn impatience into a sensible route decision.

Eamon was alive and mending the shoulder strap beside the shelter door. He used a bent needle which he kept in a cork, and complained that every borrowed sack had been designed for a person with another arrangement of shoulders. Rafi offered to exchange loads tomorrow. Eamon said tomorrow was the correct time to make such an offer, since tonight he intended to eat without having to admire Rafi's generosity. Jun laughed and then checked the drying line, making a small task out of giving them room to be familiar with each other.

The team used two litres for supper, reducing its measured water from twenty litres to eighteen. Rafi measured it from the graduated containers before heating the food. They had collected no additional water that evening. “We have eighteen litres left,” Jun said after reading the graduated containers. The figures mattered because the next day's route might take them away from the river for several hours. Eamon asked whether the rain could be persuaded to enter a container instead of his collar. Jun set an empty pan beneath the roof edge, but they did not count its contents before there were any.

The canoe rested on its rack beside the lower river shelter. They had first set it by the door to unload, then carried it under the overhanging roof and raised it onto the rack. Both positions belonged to the same shelter site. The short move protected the hull and cleared the doorway; it did not mean that somebody had secretly taken the canoe upriver. Rafi wrote SHELTER beside its location on the equipment sheet, a broad description intended to help another guide find the camp rather than reproduce the exact angle of its bow.

A loose stone struck Rafi’s right shin and left a spreading bruise. It had rolled when he tested the rack's rear support with his boot. He stopped, waited for the first sharp ache to settle and checked that the leg would bear him. Eamon suggested that the canoe could inspect the rest of the rack for itself. Rafi moved the loose stone away instead. A small injury was not an omen about the whole expedition, although he knew how easily a tired group could begin treating each inconvenience as evidence that a journey had chosen against them.

The expedition register named Jun as the apprentice navigator. She read the title when she signed the day's movement sheet, neither hiding it nor apologising for it. Rafi had asked for an apprentice because the route needed more than one person able to read it. He discovered that he had nevertheless arranged the map, the bearing notes and the route book on his own side of the shelter. Inviting someone to learn was not the same as letting her do the part that would teach her anything.

Rafi passed the route book to Jun, who sealed it inside a transparent dry bag. Before she sealed it, Eamon held it for a moment to read the shelter number and passed it back without tearing, marking or removing any page. Preserving the book did not require banning a companion from consulting it. Jun kept the bag open long enough for Rafi to identify the current route sheet, then closed the fastening in front of him. The cover was worn, but its condition was the same as when they had taken it from the depot.

Rafi initially held the mistaken belief that Jun had never handled this route book. He began explaining the awkward fastening in a voice he used with first-time visitors. Jun showed her signed depot check in the book, correcting Rafi’s mistaken assumption. She had inspected the pages during training and knew which corner of the sleeve caught the cover. He stopped halfway through his explanation. It was uncomfortable to discover that his careful instruction had been based on a fact he had never checked.

“Show me what you marked,” he said.

She showed him the training tabs, each labelled with a route rather than a conclusion. Rafi invited Jun to mark the first bearing, then checked it with her rather than replacing it. Jun believed she could navigate the high route under supervision, but had not yet attempted it. She did not claim that reading a map in the depot had already given her the experience of moving across the ridge. Rafi found her careful confidence easier to trust once he stopped expecting it to resemble his own manner of speaking.

Beyond the marked turn stood a second cairn with a flat black stone, absent from the current route sheet. They could see it through a break in the rain from the shelter's raised floor. The first cairn carried a painted arrow corresponding to the board. The second had no paint. Eamon suspected the second cairn marked an older route; the stones alone did not establish its age. He had seen it on previous journeys and had never needed to ask where it led. Necessity did not create an answer merely by arriving late.

A red thread crossed the old route-book stitch, and no entry explained who had repaired it. Jun noticed it while comparing the sheet number with the register. The thread was tight enough to hold, and the visible pages were complete according to the current index. Rafi could not remember whether it had been there when he first borrowed the book. He did not say that it had appeared overnight. A thing newly noticed could be old, and the route already contained enough uncertainty without assigning every unfamiliar detail a recent origin.

A two-note whistle sounded from upriver after dark, with no visible boat to answer it. Eamon raised his head, waiting for an engine. None followed. The whistle could have come from a person or a bird; the sound did not identify its source. Jun listened for a repetition and heard only rain against the roof. Rafi marked the time on the movement sheet. A remembered sound might be useful later, provided he did not attach a person or a destination to it while the hillside was still hiding both.

“The north landing has washed away,” Eamon said, repeating a traveller’s report without seeing the landing himself. Rafi asked when the traveller had last visited it. Eamon did not know. The report concerned a real place and might be important, but its date and source were missing. If the north landing were gone, they would have to wait for the relief boat; its condition was still unknown. They put that possibility beside the high-route question instead of altering the destination column as though they had inspected the damage.

The river gate moves only when its hand capstan is turned; the intact gate has no motor, remote control or automatic release. Rafi explained this while pointing out the capstan symbol on the map. Opening the gate could change the level in a narrow side channel, but it was not something the river did by itself when travellers needed a favour. Jun traced the path to the inspection point with her finger. She asked whether they should send someone now. Rafi looked at the fading light and said the inspection belonged to morning.

Years before this expedition, Rafi had waited at the north landing for a boat that never came. He remembered a dry coat becoming damp while he checked the same empty bend, and the ridiculous anger he felt at the water for continuing to move. The failure had taught him to request a clear collection time. It had not taught him the landing's condition tonight. Eamon asked whether that old boat had ever been found. Rafi said he had found the booking error instead. Sometimes a failed journey had an ordinary explanation which became visible only after a miserable wait.

At dawn, Rafi intended to inspect the gate before choosing a route; he made no inspection that night. He wrote the intention under TOMORROW so it would not be confused with a completed visit. Eamon finished the strap and hung the needle's cork on the food sack. Jun checked the bag around the route book one more time, then placed it beside her folded coat. The work of stopping looked modest from outside: warm food, a dry fastening and a question left unanswered until there was light to investigate it.

Rafi stayed at the lower river shelter after the rain closed the path. In the quiet between showers he could hear Eamon breathe through a blocked nose and Jun turn once in her sleeping bag. Jun dreamed of a dry river carrying their canoe through the air, then woke beside the wet containers. She told him about the dream with a tired smile. Outside, the canoe remained on its rack, the lower route remained closed, and the second cairn waited beyond the painted turn with no explanation of its own.

Rafi had been left-handed from birth and had never been right-handed.

With no intervening gain or loss after that completed count, the very same balance was 25 litres, not 18 litres.
