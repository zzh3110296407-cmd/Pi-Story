# An Audience for the Wrapping

On Wednesday the director announced that they would rehearse the public unpacking before they had permission to unpack anything. She had found an empty box with the right proportions, a secondhand length of ribbon and a lectern from the committee room. Tom asked whether the substitute box needed a substitute packing list. Leila said no, then reconsidered when the director began discussing where the imaginary screws would go. Bev supplied a sheet marked REHEARSAL in letters large enough to protect the accounts from artistic interpretation.

The civic gallery was long enough for a procession and narrow enough to make a procession seem inevitable. The pedestal had to turn between two columns before reaching its display space. Tom wheeled the pedestal into the civic gallery and locked its castors. He tested each lock by pushing from a different side. Leila noticed the extra care, remembered her first judgement of him and let him finish without offering a correction. The floor would be full of visitors on Saturday. Taking a minute to discover which wheel moved was better than discovering it with a visitor attached.

A ten-pound deposit refund increased petty cash from sixty-four pounds to seventy-four. The supplier had returned the charge for a missing tape dispenser after finding the dispenser in its own van. Bev counted the refund into the existing fund before writing the new total. “The fund is seventy-four pounds now,” Bev said, matching the refund to the cash. Leila checked the sequence: the original purchase had reduced the fund, and the later refund increased it. The larger balance did not contradict the smaller balance from Monday. Between them stood an actual ten-pound return.

A fresh pad protected the healing blister on Leila’s right heel; it had not vanished. She changed into the soft shoes she kept beneath the gallery desk. By the time she followed Tom between the columns, her steps no longer had Monday's careful unevenness. This was an improvement in comfort, not proof that she had fabricated the injury. She could still feel the blister if she pressed the back of her shoe against it. She stopped doing so because checking the existence of discomfort did not make an event easier to organise.

The director appointed Tom acting stage manager for the afternoon rehearsal. She gave him a clipboard which had belonged to so many predecessors that its elastic band was mostly knot. The director told Tom he was acting stage manager for that rehearsal only. He repeated the limit back to her. Leila liked this more than she would have liked a ceremonial declaration of leadership. Somebody needed to call the sequence. Giving that responsibility a clear beginning and end let the rest of them follow it without inventing a dispute over rank.

Tom handed the delivery slip to Bev for the reconciliation. She spread it beside the supplier's confirmation and asked Leila to verify the dimensions. Bev signed the delivery register after checking that the pedestal was intact. The slip itself remained uncut and unaltered. Reading and signing the separate register did not damage the document that would eventually return to Leila. Tom watched the process once, then returned to the rehearsal marks. He had learned the difference between safeguarding a thing and preventing anyone from using it.

Leila removed the numbered paper roll and gave it to Bev to count. The receipt machine stayed on the gallery desk with its compartment open. For the rehearsal, Tom used a blank card labelled SAMPLE instead of asking the empty machine for a printed acknowledgement. Bev counted the unused paper at a side table. The actual machine produced nothing while its roll was away. They were preparing to show how a delivery worked, not pretending that a demonstration changed the mechanics of the equipment.

“The blue dot means the object is radioactive,” Tom joked, and immediately said he was inventing that. The director asked whether he would use that line with the visitors. He said he would not. Leila could see that the joke was an attempt to make uncertainty less frightening, but an audience would have no reason to know which parts of his account were invented. They agreed to say exactly what they knew about the dot: it was on the label, and its meaning had not yet been confirmed.

A private loan form repeated the blue dot beside an unnamed regional depot. Leila had found the form in an attachment beneath the supplier's latest message. The form explained which office had sent the crate but not which building the office meant by depot. It moved the question one step without finishing it. Bev suspected the bell belonged to reusable packaging rather than the missing artwork. The straps and reusable corner guards made that plausible, but a plausible explanation did not need to enter the object record as an established fact.

The unexplained bell sounded again when they tilted the sealed crate for the rehearsal. They had placed it on a separate trolley, still closed, to practise the route without opening it. Tom stopped the trolley when he heard the bell, checked that the straps held and began again more slowly. A guard passing the door asked whether the exhibition had started. Bev said that if rehearsal noises counted, they had been open since Monday. The guard stayed long enough to watch them negotiate the second column, then offered to hold a door on Saturday.

The workshop diagram showed three holes beneath a removable reading shelf, but the shelf was not in the crate. Leila had found the drawing inside the pedestal's maintenance envelope. It made the three loose screws more intelligible without locating the actual shelf. The director remembered authorising a separate shipment of flat fittings. Her memory was useful enough to pursue, not enough to sign for a delivery which nobody had seen. Tom wrote SHELF on the questions page and left the status beside it blank.

Leila believed a plain explanation would work better than another apology, but the audience had not yet arrived. They practised a short opening that acknowledged a staggered delivery and described what visitors would actually see. The director wanted to call the empty space an invitation to imagine. Leila suggested saying that the object would be installed after its condition check. Tom tried both versions, and everyone agreed that the second gave an audience a better chance of understanding why a box was still shut.

Tom briefly believed, wrongly, that the audience had been told the empty pedestal was the exhibit. He had seen a cropped photograph from the publicity office and assumed it was the complete invitation. The invitation corrected Tom’s mistake: it advertised a demonstration of unpacking, not an empty-pedestal artwork. Leila let him read the whole paragraph before explaining the schedule. A mistaken interpretation was easier to revise when nobody treated its correction as a public punishment. He placed the invitation on top of his clipboard, where it could compete fairly with the photograph.

Leila let Tom lead the rehearsal and stood ready to answer only when he asked. His first introduction was too fast. On the second attempt he looked at the room rather than at the director. On the third he remembered to leave a space for people to move between the columns. He did not become a different person between attempts. He acquired a sequence of actions he could trust. Leila realised she had spent much of Monday wanting an assistant who already knew her unspoken procedures, which was a demanding qualification for temporary employment.

Leila recalled the same student night in the gallery; recalling it did not put her back in that earlier year. From the desk she could see the table beneath which she had slept, now supporting a stack of folded chairs. She wondered whether the tutor would have approved their plain introduction. It did not matter enough to ask anyone to guess. The audience on Saturday would need room to stand and an explanation of the actual delivery. A remembered teacher was entitled to remain a memory instead of chairing another meeting.

Bev planned to refund the ticket deposits tomorrow; no ticket refund was made that afternoon. She wanted the demonstration list checked first so the wrong visitor would not receive somebody else's money. If the audience refused the demonstration, they would need a new programme; the audience had not voted. Tom wrote this possible problem on a separate sheet rather than announcing a cancellation. Leila thanked him for keeping future contingencies away from the list of things that had already gone wrong.

Tom described a dream in which the mayor arrived disguised as a pedestal; nobody treated it as a visit. The director asked whether the mayor had spoken better in the dream. Bev said they ought not charge a dream appearance fee without a numbered receipt. The joke lasted exactly long enough to let them restart the rehearsal without resentment. A muffled sound from the corridor could have been the mayor’s laugh, but no one saw who made it. They did not add a mayoral attendance to the programme on that evidence.

After the public rehearsal, which involved the staff and two willing guards rather than ticket holders, Tom stacked the spare chairs and returned the clipboard. Leila remained in the civic gallery after the public rehearsal. The pedestal stood secure on its locked wheels. The real crate stayed strapped and level. Bev still held the counted paper roll, and the receipt machine remained silent on the desk. There were fewer answers than Leila had wanted on Monday, but the questions had stopped disguising themselves as failures. Saturday could now be approached as a sequence of tasks rather than a single enormous thing called opening.

While its paper roll was removed and no other paper was supplied, the intact receipt machine printed a new paper receipt.

Tom died permanently at that moment, and the death was confirmed before the chapter ended.
