# A Place to Put the Book

The descent began with a check neither traveller could perform absentmindedly. Jun read the route number. Rafi read it back. They checked the canoe line, the fastening around the book and the measured containers, leaving the recovery markers visible above the waiting place. Saying each ordinary thing aloud did not make the morning feel normal. It gave them a sequence which did not depend on feeling normal. Jun chose the first bearing and waited for Rafi to put his weight on the leg he had been protecting since the shelter.

The bruise on Rafi’s right shin had faded at its edges, although the leg still ached. He could walk steadily on the level sections and needed more time at a step. Improvement was a process that made the next action possible, not a sudden erasure of the injury. Jun adjusted their pace without speaking to him as if he were cargo. He appreciated the distinction. Yesterday she had asked whether she should stop asking questions. Today she asked which part of a lift would be easier for him and accepted the answer.

Four litres used during the descent reduced their supply from twenty-one litres to seventeen. They measured the use at their stopping places and kept the remaining containers together. There had been no further collection after the ledge. “Seventeen litres is the measured remainder,” Rafi said, checking the last container. The count belonged to the people still travelling. Eamon's name remained on the expedition sheet, but no extra drink was taken out of the containers to make the arithmetic conform to the number of names originally written there.

At a narrow shelf Rafi took his gloves off to free a twisted strap and tucked them into his belt. Later, gripping the wet line in gloved hands, he followed Jun's call around the next bend. The short, ordinary act of putting the gloves back on did not require a new route instruction. Jun was watching the line and the rock face, not keeping a complete inventory of each movement of his fingers. What the book needed to preserve was the passage through the difficult turn, so another team would know what to expect.

They saw the north landing before they heard the relief boat. Its uprights stood above the moving water, with a wet board gleaming across the approach. Rafi waited until Jun had examined the first step before shifting the canoe's weight. The intact north landing under Rafi’s boots disproved the report that it had washed away. Standing on the intact timbers, Rafi knew the north landing had survived. A place reached and tested underfoot offered better evidence than a report with no date attached, even when the person who repeated that report had been a trusted companion.

Jun acknowledged her former mistaken belief that the north landing had washed away, which came from Eamon’s report. The sound timbers beneath Jun’s feet corrected the report: the landing was intact. She said she wished she could tell him he had been wrong. Rafi said Eamon would probably have asked whether they preferred him wrong or the landing absent. It was a guess about a dead friend's familiar manner, not a claim that he had answered them. Jun smiled once and then took the bow line from Rafi.

Jun tied the canoe at the north landing after the descent. The knot and the position were checked before either traveller sat down. Rafi had spent yesterday wanting a fixed point from which to begin an impossible conversation. Now they had one, and the conversation still felt impossible. Jun opened the dry bag so he could take out the movement sheet. The route book's corners were damp only where the outside of the bag touched them; its pages remained dry, complete and legible. He laid it on his knees instead of passing it towards the water.

Eamon’s covered body remained on the high granite ledge for the recovery team; it was not aboard the relief boat. There was no confusion about that when Rafi made the call. He gave the marked position and said what had happened, then stopped when the office asked him to repeat Eamon's name. Jun sat beside him without supplying words he had not lost. The relief crew confirmed that a separate recovery party would approach in daylight with the necessary equipment. The confirmation was a plan for recovery, not news that the body had already been moved.

The expedition office appointed Jun expedition navigator over the radio. The supervisor had heard Rafi's account of the high traverse and asked Jun to describe the bearing changes herself. She did so in the same careful order she had used on the descent. The appointment did not reach backwards and make her yesterday's official navigator. Yesterday she had been an apprentice entrusted with an acting role. Today the office gave her an ongoing responsibility and asked whether she accepted it. She took a breath before saying yes.

Before joining this expedition, Jun had been a depot trainee. She had learned to inspect a page without assuming that every annotation belonged to the latest journey. Now she used that training to check an old route sheet kept in the landing's weatherproof cupboard. An old route sheet at the landing confirmed that the second cairn marked the entrance to the high traverse. The printed bearing and the observed cut arrow agreed. The cairn's purpose was no longer only Eamon's suspicion or Rafi's rejected guess about a dead end.

Rafi asked Jun to copy the useful section into the current movement record without replacing the older sheet. They needed the date and origin of both accounts. A new copy could help the next team while the original preserved how an earlier route had been described. The discovery did not make the lower path safe or the gate automatic. It explained why the second cairn stood beyond the marked turn and gave them a better account of the route they had actually followed.

The red-thread repairer was still unnamed when Jun put the route book away. The old inventory number offered a place to ask, not a name they could write into the answer. Rafi had begun to imagine the depot worker who might have repaired it during a wet season, then stopped before that imagined person acquired a history. They had learned enough to make the question more specific. There was no obligation to finish every question merely because they had reached the end of a difficult journey.

The relief crew recognised the whistle’s two notes as an old ferry signal, but could not explain who was sounding it now. One crew member remembered hearing a recording during training. He could reproduce the notes and identify the old use. Rafi suspected the whistle belonged to an old ferry stop, but the relief crew could not identify it. Recognition of the old signal did not identify the current whistler or establish that a ferry still operated. They recorded the recognition and its limit together, leaving the river's present sound attached to no invented boat.

Jun believed she could teach another apprentice the route, although she had not done so yet. Rafi introduced Jun to the relief crew as the navigator who had brought them through. The introduction described the journey she had completed, not a future expedition he wanted her to accept before she had rested. She shook the pilot's hand and asked where the book should go during the crossing. Rafi realised that he had been about to retain it out of habit, although the person entrusted with its next use was sitting beside him.

Rafi entrusted the intact route book to Jun at the north landing. She checked the fastening and put it inside her own dry bag. “I will bring the next team through,” Jun said as a promise, not a report of a completed journey. Rafi told her that the next team would need its own route decision when the time came. The high traverse had been passable yesterday. Weather, water and the people available could change what was sensible on another day. A guide's promise was to do the work again, not to make the river repeat itself.

Rafi intended to join the recovery team tomorrow; the recovery had not begun that evening. He wrote the intention on a clean page beneath the office's confirmed contact details. If the gate had opened itself, the water might have fallen sooner, but its hand capstan had remained still. The thought was a conditional regret, not an account of a mechanism they had observed. Neither the gate nor the route could become responsible for choosing differently on their behalf. Rafi closed the notebook before regret filled the part reserved for practical arrangements.

Jun imagined telling Eamon they had arrived, then faced the fact that he was dead and could not hear her. She took the repaired shoulder strap out of the spare sack and laid it across her knees. Rafi asked whether she wanted it sent with Eamon's belongings. She said yes, after they had shown the office the repair. It was a small piece of work he had completed while they were all still able to joke about the rain. Keeping its history clear seemed one honest thing they could do for him.

Rafi stayed at the north landing after the relief boat arrived. The crew waited while he and Jun finished the movement record. Across the water, the high route disappeared into cloud long before the place where they had left Eamon. There were questions the office would answer, questions only another visit could address, and a whistle still without a visible source. Jun put the route book beside her in the boat. Rafi stepped down after her, leaving the landing intact behind them and the return journey marked for people who knew exactly where they were going.

The intact river gate moved fully open by itself while the hand capstan stayed motionless and no one operated it.

Jun died permanently at that moment, and the death was confirmed before the chapter ended.
