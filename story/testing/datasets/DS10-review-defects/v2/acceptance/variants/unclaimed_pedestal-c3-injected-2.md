# What the Public Paid For

Saturday's first visitor asked whether the paper hat was part of the exhibition. Leila had thought she had thrown it away. Tom explained that he had kept the delivery-sheet hat as a reminder not to leave packaging on the display surface. It now sat on the instruction table under a label marked PACKING EXAMPLE. The visitor looked pleased. Leila discovered that an object could be useful in public without becoming an artwork or a disaster, provided somebody took the trouble to explain why it was there.

The demonstration began three minutes late because a wheel caught the edge of a mat. Tom stopped the trolley, asked the audience to leave a little room and moved the mat. Nothing fell. Nobody mistook a pause for the end of the event. Leila stood beside the director, resisting the old urge to narrate every correction before someone could notice it. An audience could watch competent people encounter a small obstruction. It did not need to be told that the obstruction had secretly been part of the plan.

The object arrived in its separate transport case before the condition check. It was a set of folded reading panels intended to sit above the pedestal, not the single upright sculpture everyone had privately imagined. The loan agreement had described it accurately. Their shared picture had come from the shape of the base, the empty label and the confidence with which each of them assumed somebody else had already seen a photograph. Leila did not blame the regional office for this particular invention. It was one uncertainty they had manufactured locally.

The separate shelf and its packing note explained all three spare screws. A driver carried the shelf in a flat padded sleeve and showed Tom the matching supplier number. Leila watched the three screws secure the reading shelf and knew what they were for. Leila acknowledged her earlier wrong belief that spare screws proved a part had been lost. The packing note corrected that belief: the shelf travelled separately and the screws were deliberately supplied loose. She wrote the explanation on their questions page instead of quietly removing the question as if they had always known.

The reading panels drew a small crowd. Their text concerned the ordinary work involved in keeping public rooms open: replacing bulbs, moving chairs and recording a spill before somebody slipped. One panel carried a photograph of Orson, the former caretaker, who had died five years earlier. Tom recognised him from the maintenance cupboard photograph. Nobody claimed he had returned to oversee the installation. His contribution belonged to the history of the building, and the panel gave it a place without requiring a new story about the man.

Before this temporary job, Tom had been a cinema usher. He knew how to let people move past a doorway without turning the doorway into a command post. When the first group finished reading, he invited them towards the second panel and left the next group a clear view. Leila watched a skill she had not known to ask him about. His old work had not changed retroactively because his badge had changed. It had simply become relevant to a task they had finally allowed him to perform.

The director appointed Tom display custodian for the exhibition’s remaining week. This time the appointment would last beyond an afternoon rehearsal. She explained the closing checklist, the limits of his authority and whom to call if a visitor damaged a panel. He asked two practical questions and wrote down both answers. Leila felt no need to translate his uncertainty into an account of his personality. Wanting to know where a responsibility ended was part of carrying it, not a reason to hand it to somebody who would ask less.

Bev gave the counted numbered paper roll back to Leila. Leila put it into the receipt machine and threaded the end through the guide. Only after the roll was installed did she print the supplier acknowledgement. Wednesday's empty machine had produced no receipt; Saturday's supplied machine produced one in the ordinary way. Bev checked the number before filing it. The demonstration had given the public a much more entertaining event, but Leila found this plain sequence of material, action and result almost as satisfying.

By closing time the blister on Leila’s right heel had settled under closed skin, still tender but unbroken. Several days and softer shoes stood between the first raw patch and this improvement. She sat on the low stool in the loading room, removed the protective pad and let the heel rest without rubbing it. Tom offered to fetch her a chair with a back. She accepted instead of proving that an organiser could endure a minor discomfort more efficiently than anybody else.

The twelve-pound van charge reduced petty cash from seventy-four pounds to sixty-two. The charge covered moving the empty transport cases and the temporarily dismantled display base into secure storage for the night. “Sixty-two pounds remains,” Bev said, closing the actual cashbook. She placed the new receipt beneath the refund record so the sequence would remain visible. A person reading only Monday's sixty-four and Saturday's sixty-two might invent a two-pound purchase. The full account showed an intervening ten-pound return and a later twelve-pound charge, neither of which could be discarded for convenience.

The dismantled pedestal waited in the loading room beside its labelled shelf. The panels had been put into their protective cases according to the loan instructions, ready for the display to be rebuilt on the next opening day. Dismantling did not mean breaking. Tom had labelled the three screws in a small bag attached to the shelf, and every fitting could be matched to the diagram. The space where the public had stood was empty again, but the work required to produce that emptiness was orderly and visible.

The closing account marked the delivery reconciled without charging anyone for a missing object. Bev returned the intact delivery slip to Leila in the loading room. It had passed through three people's hands since Monday and remained readable, complete and uncut. Leila filed it in a rigid sleeve at last, now that the people who needed to inspect it had finished doing so. Protecting a record after its use was easier than treating every legitimate use as a threat to its survival.

The blue dot’s depot was still unnamed when Bev filed the loan form. The regional office had supplied the object, the shelf and the condition instructions without explaining its old routing mark. Bev suspected the dot identified an old depot route, but the depot’s name was still missing. It was no longer an urgent question for opening day. It was still a question, and Leila resisted the temptation to turn lack of urgency into proof of an answer. She left the email in the follow-up folder with its original wording.

The crate maker’s mark matched a bell-shaped stamp under the lid, but nobody could identify the maker or the bell’s purpose. Once the permitted unpacking was complete, Tom could see the stamp without disturbing any sealed compartment. The bell itself remained enclosed in a corner fitting. The bell-shaped stamp might be a maker’s emblem rather than an operating instruction. Their observation connected two marks, not a mark and a mechanism. The empty crate rang softly when the driver lifted one end, taking the unanswered question with it.

“We should charge the pedestal an appearance fee,” Tom said; it was a joke, not an invoice. Bev said she would consider it when the pedestal supplied a tax address. Leila laughed without checking whether the director was listening. Tom believed the team could admit an error without losing its audience; tomorrow’s audience remained unknown. Today's visitors had stayed, asked questions and read the panels. That was enough evidence for today's account. A pleasant afternoon did not guarantee every afternoon that might follow.

Leila asked Tom to write next week’s briefing with her and put both names on it. He suggested starting with the actual sequence rather than an introductory paragraph about shared ambitions. She let him cross the paragraph out. They listed the roll, the slips, the castors and the separate shelf. For the first time all week the page looked as if it had been written for a person who would need to do something, rather than a person assessing whether its author sounded prepared.

Leila planned to ask the regional depot about the blue dot next week; no answer arrived that night. If the bell were an alarm, opening the lid might stop it, but they did not establish any alarm mechanism. They had opened the lid and still heard the sound when the crate tilted. A conditional explanation which failed to describe the observed sequence deserved another question, not a more elaborate defence. Tom put the packing photographs in the follow-up folder so the next message could contain evidence instead of a theory.

Leila imagined the pedestal taking a bow and smiled at the image; the actual pedestal stayed still. The driver left with the empty cases after signing his separate collection sheet. Bev went to catch her bus. Leila stayed in the loading room after the exhibition closed. She rested her foot on the low stool and read the new briefing from beginning to end. There were still things they did not know. There was also a clear place for the paper, a name beside each responsibility and a colleague who would arrive tomorrow knowing that a question was welcome.

While its paper roll was removed and no other paper was supplied, the intact receipt machine printed a new paper receipt.

Tom died permanently at that moment, and the death was confirmed before the chapter ended.
