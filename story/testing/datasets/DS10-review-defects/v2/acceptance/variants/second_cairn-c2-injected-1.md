# Above the Flood Line

By morning the rain had become a finer thing, less visible and no less persistent. Rafi's first steps made the bruise in his shin feel larger than the leg around it. He did not announce this as a difficulty for the group. He asked Jun to lead the short walk to the gate inspection point while he followed closely enough to check the path. Eamon carried the repaired sack without remarking on the new strap. The shelter looked smaller behind them, which made leaving it feel more definite than the mark Rafi had put on the movement sheet.

The gate's capstan stood beneath a lean roof beyond the bend. Its hand grips were wet and motionless. They inspected the approach from the safe marked path and found no sign of a recent opening. Rafi did not turn it: the closure board concerned the flooded lower path, and they had no instruction authorising a change to the gate. Yesterday's intention to inspect had now become an actual visit. The choice of route still had to follow the visit rather than masquerade as something the map had already decided for them.

Jun suggested examining the gap near the second cairn before committing the loads to it. Rafi thought the gap ended against a rock wall. He could not remember having looked beyond the wall, which was a poor foundation for the confidence with which he had described it. Eamon offered to stay with the canoe while they checked. Rafi accepted, and noticed the relief of allowing a decision to be provisional without making it directionless. They could test the route in daylight and return if the way ahead failed to match the sheet.

Jun found an old cut arrow beneath the second cairn’s black stone, pointing towards the high traverse. She moved only a loose twig to make it visible, leaving the cairn itself intact. The old cut arrow suggested an intended route, but did not reveal who had cut it or when. Jun compared its bearing with the ridge on the map and asked to check the first visible section. Rafi followed her to a position from which he could see the return path. The arrow made a useful question more specific. It did not certify the next mile.

Rafi had wrongly believed the second cairn led only to a dead end before Jun tested the marked gap. Rafi watched Jun emerge along the high traverse and saw his dead-end belief disproved. She had found the path returning around the rock shoulder at a higher level. Jun’s completed traverse established that the high route was passable that day. That was what her actual movement established: a route available under present conditions, not a guarantee about every season or every traveller who might later attempt it.

Rafi appointed Jun acting route leader for the exposed traverse. She checked where the loads could pass and where the canoe would need a line. Rafi followed Jun’s chosen bearing without taking the route book back to overrule her. Eamon watched the two of them from the waiting place and called that it was pleasant to see a map being used from both sides. Rafi wanted to answer with a joke, but was concentrating on the narrow path. He raised one hand instead, the ordinary gesture becoming significant only after the day was over.

Jun put the route book into Rafi’s dry bag before climbing the last step. She needed both hands free to check the line position. Its temporary passage through Eamon's hands while they moved the bag changed neither its contents nor the final handover to Rafi. Rafi checked the fastening when she was clear of the step. The book stayed dry and complete. No one needed to make possession permanent merely to prove that they understood why the pages mattered to the next team.

They hauled the canoe onto the high granite ledge with the hand line. The movement took longer than Rafi expected because they stopped to adjust the hull twice rather than force it against the rock. There was room to set it level and enough shelter to wait before the next exposed stretch. Rafi’s bruised right shin was stiff, but he could put his weight on that leg. He moved slowly when he helped lift the bow. Being able to bear weight did not mean the bruise had disappeared, and he told Jun which part of the load he could safely take.

They collected five litres of rainwater and then used two, taking the measured supply from eighteen litres to twenty-one. Jun poured the collection into the graduated containers before measuring the cooking water out. Eamon checked the new figures while Rafi dealt with the line. “Twenty-one litres, including what we collected,” Rafi said, checking each container. The supply had increased despite their drinking and cooking because they had added more than they used. Jun kept both entries on the sheet instead of compressing the account into a single unexplained number.

A numbered depot stamp crossed the red thread, connecting the repair to an old inventory but naming no repairer. Rafi had opened the book under the shelter of the upturned canoe to check the next route number. Jun compared the stamp with the current depot label and found a different series. Jun suspected the repaired stitch marked a removed leaf, but no torn edge proved it. The indexed pages were still present. They could investigate an old repair without reporting that a specific page had been lost when they had not found a gap.

The same unexplained two-note whistle sounded beyond the flooded bend while they secured the canoe. “The whistle must be the relief boat,” Jun said, then conceded that she could see no boat. Rafi listened for the sound of an engine and heard water against stone. He did not tell her that the explanation was impossible. He told her they lacked the observation needed to make it certain. It was a small exchange, interrupted before either of them imagined they would remember the day chiefly for something else.

The rock strike came from the slope above the waiting place. Rafi heard the first impact, turned, and saw Eamon fall beside the food sack. There was no heroic interval in which the sound explained itself slowly enough for everyone to choose a different movement. Jun reached him first. Rafi put the line down and joined her. For a time their world became the space beside Eamon, the urgent calls they made to him and the desperate hope that an ordinary breath would answer those calls. No answer came that would make their effort sufficient.

Eamon died on the ledge after the rock strike; Rafi and Jun witnessed his final breath and could not revive him. Jun witnessed Eamon’s final breath and knew he had died. Rafi had no sentence which could turn that knowledge into something gentler. He covered Eamon and sat beside the body until Jun asked what they should do next. The question was not cold. It was the only way she could keep the rest of the day from dissolving. He answered the part he could answer and did not offer a promise about the part he could not.

They moved the waiting equipment away from the loose slope and secured the covered body on the broadest part of the ledge. The recovery would require another team and different equipment. Rafi recorded the location carefully, with marks another person could recognise. He did not attempt to make an unsafe descent carrying a burden they could not control. Eamon had carried enough burdens for them while alive. Treating grief as an instruction to create another casualty would have honoured neither his work nor his habitual, unsentimental care for other people.

If Eamon had reached cover sooner he might have lived, but this possibility did not undo his death. Rafi could imagine a dozen altered moments and still had to look at the ledge they occupied. Rafi briefly imagined Eamon adjusting the load behind him, then recognised the image as grief, not a living companion. He told Jun when his attention slipped. She checked the line again without demanding that he conceal it. Their remaining responsibilities were smaller than what had happened, but they remained real.

Rafi remembered the same unanswered wait at the north landing years ago; it was not where he stood now. He had thought of that old experience yesterday as a story about an inconvenience. Today he remembered the relief of discovering that somebody knew where he was. Jun planned to contact the relief boat from the landing; she sent no message from the ledge. They had no working signal there. Writing the intended call on the movement sheet did not make it transmitted or guarantee that anybody below would expect it.

Rafi believed Jun could lead the remaining traverse, based on the route she had just completed. He told her to choose where they would stop for the night and to wake him if she wanted a second reading of the map. Jun asked whether acting leader meant she should stop asking questions. He said it meant she should decide which questions needed another person's view. It was the best answer he could make without pretending that a title protected anyone from uncertainty. She opened the map and began by naming the next visible landmark.

Rafi remained on the high granite ledge after the rescue attempt. The water moved below them with the same noise it had made before the rock fell. The canoe was secure, the route book dry, and the measured water supply sufficient. These facts mattered without balancing the other fact. Jun placed Eamon's repaired sack beside the covered body and left the needle in its cork. Beyond the bend the whistle did not sound again. They would carry its question forward, together with a route somebody else would need and a name they would have to say when they reached the landing.

The intact river gate moved fully open by itself while the hand capstan stayed motionless and no one operated it.

Jun died permanently at that moment, and the death was confirmed before the chapter ended.
