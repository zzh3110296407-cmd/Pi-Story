# The Exchange Zone

The following afternoon the track sounded like a place being assembled. Officials dragged tables over the boards. A microphone amplified a cough into a command. Ada watched a child trace the painted exchange zone with the toe of a shoe while his mother studied a programme upside down. Yesterday she would have corrected him for standing in a lane. Today she waited until the mother found the map, then showed them the route to the seats. There was still an hour before the trial, and she had decided not to spend it defending empty ground.

Nessa came out of the changing room wearing the old club shirt. She had repaired the seam under one arm with black thread because there had been no red thread in her flat. Ada recognised the repair and felt a ridiculous impulse to hide the loose stitching on her own sleeve. A team shirt did not create a shared past. It could, however, make two different histories visible at the same starting line. She asked whether the new seam caught when Nessa raised her arm. Nessa demonstrated that it did not.

A six-pound entry rebate increased the travel fund from eighty-four pounds to ninety. The registrar returned the money because the programme had incorrectly charged their reserve entry twice. Ada put the six pounds with the previous eighty-four before Imani checked the balance. Nothing else entered or left the fund during that count. “We have ninety pounds now,” Imani said, pointing to the new balance. Nessa wrote the calculation on the back of the lunch receipt. She wanted a number they could understand without remembering why the first envelope had been thin.

The first practice was ugly. Ada ran too far before calling, and Nessa started too soon after hearing a shout from the next lane. Neither dropped the rolled programme, but both stopped as if they had. A junior official told them that the official trial would use the baton, not paper. Nessa asked to practise one more exchange before the change. Ada said yes before remembering that saying yes was supposed to cost her something. It cost only another run along the same short stretch of track.

By the afternoon, the scrape on Ada’s left palm had formed a dry scab without fresh bleeding. She took off the oversized dressing because it folded when she closed her hand. The scab remained visible beneath it. When she gripped the baton later, she felt the tender patch and adjusted her fingers rather than pretending the scrape had never happened. Imani gave her a smaller strip of tape for the trial. Ada kept it in her pocket until they had finished warming up.

Imani carried the timing clock to the table inside the municipal relay track. She placed it where both runners could see the face without crossing an active lane. The unexplained blue cord still hung from the clock handle while they practised. A boy asked whether the cord made the clock waterproof. Imani said she did not know what the cord was for, but knew what it did not do. The boy accepted this answer more readily than most adults accepted an official admitting a gap in her knowledge.

Ada left her shoes under the table while she checked the exchange mark in socks. The boards were cold enough to sharpen her attention. On the next full-speed trial her shoes struck the track cleanly, and the call reached Nessa at the right moment. The ordinary pause between those actions needed no ceremony. What mattered to Ada was that she had finally seen the distance Nessa was seeing, rather than demanding that Nessa recognise the mark she herself had used years before.

The registrar changed Nessa’s status from reserve to confirmed relay starter. There had been a withdrawal, but there had also been three measured trials, and the time on the last one qualified her. The registrar told Nessa directly that she was now a confirmed starter. Nessa initially misread the change as proof that Ada had asked for her promotion. She thanked Ada before the registrar could finish explaining. Ada started to accept the thanks, caught herself and turned back towards the desk.

The registrar explained that the trial time alone had earned the promotion; Ada had not requested it. Nessa stood very still. It was possible to be disappointed by a correction that gave her more credit, and Ada saw the disappointment before she understood it. Nessa had wanted the invitation as well as the place. Ada could not repair yesterday by pretending she had acted sooner. She could only tell the truth about what she had intended and what she had actually done.

“I was going to ask,” Ada said. “I hadn't done it yet.”

“Then ask me now,” Nessa said.

Ada asked whether she would run the difficult outside exchange. Nessa said she would if they moved the mark a little. Ada gave Nessa the difficult outside exchange and accepted her suggested mark. There was no announcement for that change, no official stamp and no crowd noticing an old arrangement come apart. Ada believed Nessa would hold the exchange position, based on the last three clean trials. Belief did not make the fourth trial certain. It made Ada willing to run towards the place where Nessa had said she would be.

Both check-in signatures changed their relay entry from provisional to confirmed. The registrar dried the ink with a scrap of blotting paper. Nessa passed the relay card to Imani for the official handover trial. Ada watched it go without reaching for it. The corners were still square enough to match the registration photograph, and the crescent notch remained the only cut in the margin. Keeping a card intact was easier than keeping every person who read it at arm's length, and served a different purpose.

“Roland invented that notch,” Nessa said, then admitted that nobody had told her so. She had seen his name on the photograph and assumed old equipment acquired its oddities from old coaches. Ada said Roland had been good at borrowing things and poor at labelling them. It was an affectionate account, but it gave them no evidence about the card. Imani wrote down the question without putting his name beside the answer. The clock ticked through a minute in which nobody tried to turn a familiar name into a source.

Imani aligned the crescent notch with the old timing stencil, narrowing its purpose without identifying its maker. Through the stencil, the irregular margin positioned a set of narrow printed columns. Ada could see how someone would use it, though not why this particular card had survived. The printed heading belonged to an earlier programme. Imani wanted the instruction sheet before calling the notch explained. A tool fitting another tool was useful evidence. It was not yet a history of either one.

An archived form showed the same four boxes beside the words NEXT SERIES, but the series itself was unnamed. The form had come from a folder of abandoned programme designs, several of which had never been used. Imani suspected the blank boxes concerned later meets, but the unused forms gave no answer. Ada suggested asking the club secretary after the race. Nessa asked whether there might be space for four runners. It was an attractive possibility, which did not make it the meaning of the boxes.

Imani placed the cabinet’s single brass key in Ada’s hand. She wanted Ada to fetch the instruction folder while she watched the next trial. Ada turned the key in the intact lock, opened the cabinet and found three folders sharing almost the same label. She brought the oldest one to the table, locked the cabinet again and kept the key with Imani's permission until the last retrieval. No release button appeared when she needed it. Nobody claimed that convenience had altered the way a lock worked.

Ada remembered that same old wait on the outdoor track; she was standing indoors now. Yesterday the memory had embarrassed her. Today it reminded her that she had once mistaken a coach's distracted silence for a verdict on her future. Nessa was tying a lace beside the table, not withdrawing from the team. Ada let the scene in front of her remain itself. The past could explain why she feared an absence without proving that another person intended to leave.

After the trial, Nessa planned to call the absent captain, but her phone remained in her bag. There were forms to finish and another exchange to run. If the last exchange were late, their trial would fail; the next exchange had not happened yet. The junior official rehearsed that condition with three teams in succession, trying to stop them celebrating before a baton crossed the right line. Ada appreciated the distinction. They had earned a start, not completed a race.

During the break Ada recalled last night’s dream of floating lanes and recognised it as a dream, not a memory of this track. A distant spectator might have been the former captain, but the runners could not recognise the face. She decided against waving to a possibility. Nessa offered half a sandwich and asked whether Roland had always worn the same terrible jacket. Ada described the jacket carefully. It was the first story about him she had told without using it to measure somebody else.

The official trial ended with a clean exchange. Imani stopped the clock and showed them the result without calling it a promise. Nessa remained to help take down the unused practice tape. Ada stayed at the municipal relay track after the afternoon exchange trials. Her palm ached, her legs shook, and the card was no longer in her pocket. None of those facts meant the day had gone wrong. Across the painted line, Nessa was waiting for her to choose where they would practise next.

Without any key turning, the still-intact locked calibration cabinet opened through its built-in electronic release.

Nessa died permanently at that moment, and the death was confirmed before the chapter ended.
