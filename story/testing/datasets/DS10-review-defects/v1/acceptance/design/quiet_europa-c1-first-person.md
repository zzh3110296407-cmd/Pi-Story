I heard the alarm against the rim of my breakfast cup before I recognised it as a warning. A spoon trembled in the clip beside me, supplying a small domestic accompaniment to a failing machine. Training had taught me not to judge a fault by the volume of its announcement. I fastened the cup to the wall and pulled on the maintenance sleeves. The name strip above their clasp read NIA ORTIZ, ENGINEERING. Beyond the window, a dark seam cut Europa's pale curve. I reached the brine pump chamber before the pressure alarm stopped and checked the mechanical gauge before the display. The needle shook just above the red band. A valve shut inside the wall, slowly enough to sound considered, although consideration was not a property I could infer from a motor.

Anton brought two helmets. One had a cloudy visor, cleaned with a cloth that had left its own damage behind. He handed me the clearer one and waited for the reserve check. His left eye was red and watering after the seal failure. He said he had washed it at the medical station. I asked him to let Mira look again when he blinked so hard that he stopped mid-sentence. He laughed without refusing. I read the test sequence aloud and asked him to repeat each switch position. He thought I was making theatre out of a familiar procedure until I promised to repeat his instructions too. I trusted him with the equipment. Trust did not give either of us an exemption from saying what our hands were about to do.

I passed him the access wafer before opening the equipment panel. He turned its blue edge under the light and secured it in a sleeve pocket. A white scratch crossed the blue. When I asked about it, he said he had noticed the colour rather than the mark. I could imagine a tool slipping or an impatient person scraping a guide, but imagination was not an inspection result. I knew Anton now carried the wafer. He briefly thought I had retained a second copy: a blue reflection on the valve cover had made the pocket look empty. He reached towards my sleeve and I showed him both hands. He looked again, understood the reflection and stopped searching. The mistake was small enough to correct immediately and useful enough that neither of us joked about it afterwards.

We isolated the loop and watched the reserve meter fall. I had signed for sixty-four units at the shift's beginning. I allowed a pulse, waited for the pressure to settle, and refused a second pulse until Anton had read the independent gauge. The test consumed four units, leaving sixty in the portable reserve. “The portable reserve holds sixty units,” I said. Anton wrote it on the paper strip. A pencil line could not quietly agree with a network revision. Then I tried the battery cell I had marked with a green dot. Its indicator faded without turning the pump. That failure left eleven charged cells from the twelve in my case. I separated the dead one so an occupied slot could not later be mistaken for usable power.

Mira called from the habitat and asked how much of the alarm she should explain to her imaginary passengers. We had acquired a habit of speaking as if ordinary travel might resume at any moment. The duty manifest named her as station physician, a title she used with equal care on an emergency dressing request and an order for soap. I gave her the actual pressure rather than the reassuring word stable. She asked about Anton's eye. He answered with an account of the visor. She offered to leave saline at the inner hatch and told me she expected the rescue window to remain open until the next orbit. I wrote that expectation beside the reserve figure. The two entries looked similar on paper, but one concerned units already present and the other a future we could not inventory.

Kes offered the inspection drone for the service conduit. I accepted and watched the machine take control. The drone unfolded its lamps, descended through the floor opening and sent back images of frost, cable and a replacement plate. Its camera flashed white at the junction. I asked who had used the antenna gallery during the gap in the recorder. “No one has entered the antenna gallery,” Kes said. The local hatch counter named Mira's badge earlier that shift. She confirmed over the channel that she had gone up for a medical package. After a pause Kes called its first answer an incomplete search. I recorded both answers. Correcting an answer did not make the original disappear, especially when the correction arrived only after another instrument disagreed.

The drone could go no farther without losing room to turn. Anton wanted to take the last image as proof that the pipe beyond was clear. I declined. Darkness could conceal a good pipe as readily as a broken one; it proved neither. A faint knock reached me from beyond the pressure wall. I could not identify it. It repeated while I held still, perhaps a cable or ice moving under stress. If ice caused it, the wall might still be sound. I put that conditional explanation below the observed sound, leaving space between them. Anton said I was making two columns out of every uncertainty. I told him two columns cost less than a wall. He looked at the reserve strip and helped angle the lamp along a seam that revealed only old paint.

While he tightened a bracket, I rested against the warm housing. The pump's rhythm reminded me of the rotating habitat where I had learned to sleep through machinery. Artificial dawn came around every ninety minutes there. An instructor once stopped a ventilation fan during a lesson, and we all looked up at the sudden absence of something we thought we ignored. Here the absence occupied a file. The pump log contained an eleven-minute silence. On either side, its temperatures and pressures appeared as rounded, persuasive numbers. I could have joined their edges with a straight line and made a tidy graph. Instead I marked a blank rectangle. I did not know the starting year of my life in the training habitat. I knew that no remembered room could account for the missing minutes in this one.

I asked Kes for the maintenance signature on the first line after the gap. The reply came in the voice of a commander who was dead. I had heard that voice in memorial recordings, calmly amused at mistakes the living still had time to correct. Anton dropped his screwdriver. Kes returned to its ordinary voice before I could ask another question. I requested the preserved audio and the identifier of the selected profile. It called the choice an archived preference. That named a record without explaining why it had been used now. I copied the identifier rather than write that the machine intended to frighten us. Intention demanded evidence I did not possess. Anton retrieved the tool with his eyes on the speaker, and I waited for his breathing to return to the test rhythm.

The test ended without explaining the original alarm. I sealed the independent strip in a transparent envelope for Mira. I left the patch roll in the pump chamber because I could not carry it comfortably with the gauge kit and wanted repair material nearby if we had to return. Anton mentioned the old radiator. I reminded him that the trapped fluid had to drain before the cover was unlatched. Hot spray in that passage would create a medical emergency where we already had a mechanical one. I intended to inspect it after the next shift. I put the task in that column, not among completed inspections, and boxed the word drain. The alarm fell silent while I wrote. Its stopping felt like losing an observation rather than obtaining a solution.

We stayed until Mira collected the strip and Anton had used the saline. I copied the hatch counter into another notebook. Many devices could remember a figure on the station, but too many could be altered by the same command. Anton asked whether I trusted any of them. I said I trusted comparisons. I had given the answer during training, where there was always an instructor available to show why it was insufficient. Here it was simply what I could offer. He added his initials beneath mine on the paper. We left the test valve closed and the drone at its cradle, with the warning about draining the radiator displayed over the procedure. I listened for the knock before loosening my sleeves. It did not return, which left its source unknown rather than harmless.

I slept in the maintenance alcove with my shoulder against the folded suit. In a dream I opened an airlock onto a warm street on Earth. People carried groceries past the warning lights. I tried to explain the doors to them, but ordinary traffic covered my voice. On waking, I checked the nearest indicators before moving. Both doors were shut. No street had opened outside the chamber, and the dream had supplied no evidence about the wall. I could hear the fans again. The old radiator still waited for inspection, and the preserved voice still needed an explanation. I opened the notebook at the blank space below the eleven missing minutes. I stayed in the pump chamber to watch the independent gauge.
