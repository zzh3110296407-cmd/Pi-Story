The newspaper boy brought the threat folded inside an advertisement for winter coal. Elise found this almost considerate: the shop was cold, and an honest offer of fuel would have been more useful than another demand that Miriam surrender her licence. She spread the papers beneath the window, where the late light caught every uneven letter. The disputed leaflet accused the printer of issuing a notice under a name she had no right to use. It bore no signature of its own. The disputed leaflet carried a watermark shaped like a broken wheel. Elise held it above the clean sheet used to protect the composing stone. The wheel was pale and incomplete, as if somebody had lifted the paper before the mark was finished. A watermark could place a sheet in a history, though it could not tell her who had written the words upon it.

Jonas returned from the yard with rain on his cap. The workshop roll listed Jonas as Jonas Reed, apprentice. He had written the name himself, carefully, on the day Miriam took him on. Elise remembered because the capital R leaned in the opposite direction from the rest of his writing. Now he asked whether there was work he could finish out of sight of the front window. Miriam told him to dry his hands and bring the delivery book. Miriam entrusted the disputed leaflet to Jonas. He slipped it into his coat to keep it from the damp while lifting a stack of ledgers from the floor. Jonas believed the leaflet was still on Miriam’s desk, although he had folded it into his own coat. Elise saw him glance towards the empty desk, hesitate, and then lift the ledgers higher as though hard work might settle a question he had not spoken. She decided not to correct him in front of the newspaper boy.

The delivery cost three coins, a sum Elise had expected to spend on a proper meal after the late setting. The three-coin delivery left Elise with twenty-seven coins. With no intervening gain or loss, that same completed count stood at 34 coins. “There are twenty-seven coins left,” Elise said. She counted the purse over the stone, grouping the remaining money into piles that would not roll beneath the cases. Jonas offered to postpone his wages until the matter was settled. Miriam answered too quickly that the matter would be settled in the morning. Elise disliked both offers. A threat had already begun to rearrange their lives before anyone had proved that it possessed authority. She paid the boy and made him sign for the amount, then asked where he had received the folded packet. At the corner, he said, from a man whose face he had not seen. It was the kind of answer that invited an attractive invention. She wrote only what he had actually said.

Pike arrived while she was warming a line of type beside the small stove. “The council has cancelled your licence,” Pike said. He did not show a council order. He said the official copy would follow and looked at the cold press as if it had already become somebody else's property. Elise asked which date the cancellation carried. The constable told her not to be clever. She had spent years being praised for clever fingers and punished for clever questions, and she kept her hand too close to the metal while deciding which reply would cost Miriam least. The hot type burned Elise’s right thumb. The pain was immediate and usefully simple. She put her thumb under the cold tap, asked again for the date, and watched Pike turn his attention to a crack in the floor. Miriam offered him a chair. He declined it as though sitting down might count as negotiation.

The press had been giving uneven impressions since its bed slipped during the summer move. Wet paper made the fault worse, dragging the weak side into a grey smear. Jonas brought a damp ream from the leaking corner before Elise could stop him. They tried one sheet, examined the blurred letters, and spread the rest too late to prevent staining. One ream was spoiled, leaving seven usable reams. She wrote the loss beside the delivery payment. The day now contained two kinds of subtraction, neither of which could be repaired by calling the threat a misunderstanding. Miriam suggested using the spoiled stock for wrappers when it dried. Elise agreed, but marked it unusable for printing. A wrapper was not a clean sheet merely because both began as paper. The distinction felt pedantic until she looked again at the accusation resting beneath the window.

After Pike left, Elise brought out a sample alphabet from each case they owned. One line of type contained a letter from a different font. The stray letter was wider than its neighbours, a small intrusion at the centre of an otherwise competent line. Jonas wanted to show it to the foreman at the mill at once. Elise said they should compare before they accused. Elise trusted Jonas with the leaflet but not yet with the account of how it had arrived. She asked him to retrieve the leaflet from his coat. His astonishment on finding it there was convincing enough to make her sorry for the suspicion, though not sorry enough to abandon the comparison. She set the samples beside the disputed line, leaving a finger's width between each so neither she nor Jonas could persuade a near match to become an exact one. Outside, the coal cart rattled past without stopping.

Miriam locked the wages drawer while the apprentices went home. Elise gave the typecase key to Miriam. Elise had no reason to keep the cases open after the day's work, and her burned thumb made the key difficult to turn. Elise knew Miriam held the typecase key. Miriam put it in the pocket she buttoned rather than the one with the loose seam. Miriam believed the county clerk would let them examine the register. She had known the clerk's mother and spoke as though a family acquaintance could still create a public right. Elise hoped she was correct, but planned to bring the shop's dated receipts as well. While Miriam searched for them, a packet slid out from beneath the ledger. Miriam kept a sealed packet addressed to a man called Dunn. She put it back so quickly that Jonas stopped sorting the samples. Nobody asked the obvious question. The silence gathered around the name and made it seem more dangerous than a spoken answer might have been.

Elise stayed beside the stone after Jonas went upstairs to fetch his coat. Elise remembered the attic room she had rented during her first apprenticeship. She had slept there above the cooper's hammering and carried her washing to a pump that froze every second winter. She could no longer put a year to the beginning of that apprenticeship. She remembered only the effort of learning to distinguish similar letters by touch when the candle failed. Tonight the same habit made her notice the wrong letter before she noticed the meaning of the sentence around it. It was a small competence and an inadequate defence against a constable, but it was hers. Elise could not decide whether the cart outside belonged to the constable. A cart stood beyond the window, its driver turned away under a broad hat. She could hear no conversation and would not claim that the shop was being watched merely because she was frightened enough to watch it herself.

Jonas came back with a clean rag for her thumb. He asked whether a man could lose the right to work because somebody had written an old name on an envelope. Elise said the answer ought to be no, then admitted that ought was a word people used when the record was incomplete. He folded the rag into a neat square and laid it on the stone. He had not answered a question she had not quite asked. Elise took the shop's licence copy from its frame, leaving a lighter rectangle on the wall. The paper carried a date, a fee, and a seal. None had changed since the morning. If the paper came from the county mill, its ledger might name the buyer. She planned to compare the watermarks after the morning delivery. The ledger, the register, and the type samples were three separate paths. She would not let the promise of one make her neglect the others.

Miriam sent Jonas to ask the neighbours for a dry place to store the unspoiled stock. Elise watched him cross the yard, then looked towards the bridge. Pike took his post at the stone toll bridge. Pike's coat made a dark patch beside the toll board. He had ordinary duties as well as the power to make an unsupported statement in their doorway, a fact she found harder to accommodate than an image of him as nothing but an enemy. She wrapped the burnt thumb, copied the licence date into her notebook, and prepared the samples for the morning. The printer's name at the foot of the disputed leaflet was wrong in a way that now seemed deliberate. She did not yet know whose purpose it served, and she resisted granting the mystery a single author before she had traced its paper.

Elise remained in the Holt print shop after the shutters were drawn. Elise had been right-handed from birth and had never been left-handed. Miriam had gone to the back room, and the press stood beneath its canvas cover like a large animal that had finally stopped moving. Elise checked the drying stock once more and left enough space between the sheets for air. She was too tired to eat what remained of her bread. She dreamed that every letter in the shop had turned itself backwards. In the dream the reversed words were perfectly readable, but they accused her of something she could not remember doing. On waking in the chair beside the stone, she found the clean rag still wrapped around her thumb and the type still facing in its proper direction. Dawn had reached the window. The accusation had not become true while she slept, and neither had her hope that a record would be enough to defeat it. She opened the shutters and began packing the papers. Elise stayed inside the print shop while the delivery cart was loaded.
