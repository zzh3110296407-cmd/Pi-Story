I stand with ink close enough to stain my sleeve. I want a line that can be read without asking a frightened person to supply what it leaves out. The clean sheet came off the press so quietly that Elise thought for a moment the type had failed to touch it. She lifted the corner and saw the heading hold its edge against the pale paper. They had waited for the stock to dry, tightened the damaged bed, and rejected two trial sheets before attempting the public copy. There was no miracle in the impression, only the accumulation of small refusals to hurry. Elise carried the first clean correction to the market square. The square was filling with carts diverted from the broken bridge. A notice about the collapse hung beside a notice about the price of flour. Elise waited for a space on the public board large enough to hold the correction without covering either. Truth did not become more public by making another useful fact disappear.

The council had heard the evidence that morning. Miriam retained her existing rights, and Elise had applied under her own name after the clerk pointed out that years of work did not automatically enter a printer on the register. The council register entered Elise as a licensed master printer. She had expected triumph and felt instead the weight of the title, a responsibility too late to protect them from yesterday's rumour and early enough to govern what she printed tomorrow. The clerk gave her a numbered copy. She read the number back before signing. She paid four coins for the public notice and had twenty-eight left. “Twenty-eight coins remain,” Elise said. The payment left a smaller purse and a document she could show anyone who asked which authority permitted her work. A man waiting behind her muttered that a printed correction was merely another printed claim. She agreed and showed him the register reference at its foot.

Jonas came from the shop carrying the shop's retained proofs in a dry envelope. Elise placed the typecase key in Jonas’s hand. Elise asked him to put the dated copies in the locked case when he returned. He said he would keep the disputed names visible in the index, not file the matter under a comforting general heading. Elise trusted Jonas to keep the proofs without hiding inconvenient names. She had no promise that he would never conceal another painful detail. Trust meant allowing him a duty whose performance she could see. Jonas believed the apprentices would keep their own dated copies. He had begun to speak of the younger boys as apprentices rather than rivals, which made the new responsibility sound less like a favour she had done him. Jonas returned to the Holt print shop to unlock the cases. Elise watched him turn towards the side street, his shoulders held at a different angle from the previous morning. She could not tell whether relief or fear was moving him faster, and decided she did not need to know before letting him go.

I keep the shape of the letter in my mind. I can hear the street beyond the window while I wait for the paper to tell me less than a rumour promises. Miriam brought the sealed packet at last, opened in Jonas's presence before they left the shop. “I kept the packet because I was afraid of losing him,” Miriam said. The admission had cost her more than she had expected. She had hoped to defend her silence as loyalty and discovered that the same word had served Jonas as an excuse to deny his name. The packet contained Jonas’s mother’s apprenticeship request under the name Dunn. His mother had asked the printer to take him in under a name that would spare him the mill owner's enmity. Jonas had believed the packet was an accusation, but it contained his mother’s request for an apprenticeship. The request was a record of somebody trying to provide a future, not a proof that every choice made to protect him had been right. Miriam allowed Elise to copy the relevant passage while keeping the original in its envelope. They agreed the public correction needed the printing evidence; Jonas's family history belonged in it only where he chose to make it relevant.

The workers' secretary arrived with a folder and two witnesses. The workers’ society accepted the disputed leaflet as evidence. The original would remain available for comparison rather than disappear into a stove once it had become inconvenient. Elise showed the witnesses the watermark under the light and then showed them the dated ledger copy. The retired watermark and dated ledger identified the paper stock as older than the licence period named in the accusation. The stock had been made before the licence period named in the accusation. That fact did not alone identify every person who later handled a sheet, so the correction did not pretend otherwise. Elise knew the accusation had relied on paper older than Miriam’s licence. The accusation’s printing date remained unproved by the watermark alone. She printed the dates side by side, trusting readers with the comparison instead of demanding belief in her character. The secretary signed for the leaflet and wrote where anyone could inspect it during the society's open hours.

The wrong letter had led them to a private case kept by the former mill owner. His estate inventory listed the wider sorts, and the county sample carried the same chipped edge. The mismatched letter belonged to the former mill owner’s private press. Miriam had recognised the tilt because she had seen the owner's notices years before, not because the letter had lived in their own shop. Elise explained this without making recognition itself a fault. The mistake lay in allowing recognition to become certainty without a measurement. The mixed font did not prove who had set the disputed line on a particular day. It did establish that the shop's own cases were not its only possible source. She chose a restrained sentence for the correction and rejected the secretary's suggestion that a stronger adjective would make people read it. A stronger adjective would not make the case fit their measurements more closely.

The dated proof settled a smaller, more personal doubt. The dated proof showed Miriam had prepared a correction before the threat arrived. Miriam had prepared it and then hidden it with the packet because she feared that publishing the names would drive Jonas away. She had not invented the correction after Pike's arrival. Elise asked why she had been willing to let the shop suffer rather than show it. Miriam said she had mistaken waiting for kindness. There was no useful reply that would repair the lost day, so Elise helped her put the documents in the order in which they had been made. Miriam stood beside Elise in the market square. People who had ignored the printer that morning now stopped to ask her what the seal meant. She answered the question patiently, sometimes handing them the frame so they could examine it without leaning over Elise's bandaged hand.

I watch the edge of the sheet lift in the light. I am trying to keep a measured width separate from the name somebody wants me to accuse. The correction consumed more stock than the urgent first notice had. They needed copies for the board, the council, the workers' room, and customers who would otherwise hear only the first rumour. The correction used three reams, leaving six. Elise wrote the final count in the delivery book and made clear which three reams had become finished copies. The seven from the first evening had gained two dry replacements before losing these three; the spoiled ream never re-entered the usable total. She thought of the cold attic where a travelling compositor had once shown her how to listen before striking a loose letter into place. Elise remembered an itinerant compositor teaching her to listen for a loose sort, at a time she could not date. She could remember the sound of his teaching better than the year or even the spelling of his name. Not every useful past fact came with a convenient date, and she left the date blank rather than supplying one that felt plausible.

At the edge of the gathering, a man in a dark hat stood without taking a sheet. Elise could not know whether the silent man at the edge of the crowd had read the notice. He might have been unable to read, unwilling to carry evidence, or simply waiting for someone who had gone to buy flour. Elise disliked how readily fear made the first two possibilities into accusations. She asked the newspaper boy to leave extra copies at the counter instead of following the man. If the public kept the correction, the false leaflet might lose its authority. A correction could be kept, ignored, argued over, or wrapped around a parcel. Printing it made those futures possible without deciding which would occur. She intended to teach the apprentices to keep every dated proof. She would teach the apprentices to preserve dated proofs because records had helped them, not because records made every reader fair. The boy folded a copy into his pocket, this time with the heading facing inward to protect it from the rain.

I feel the roughness of the proof under my hand. I am here with the work unfinished, and the next impression has not yet been made. Miriam asked whether the shop window should be repainted to include Elise's new title. Elise said they could discuss it after the accounts were copied. There had been enough permanent decisions made under pressure. They returned to the narrow street, where Jonas had opened the cases and laid the proof envelope on the dry stone. He showed them the index before asking about supper. Nothing had been filed under scandal or vindication. There were dates, names, document numbers, and a short note directing the reader from Reed to Dunn. Elise touched the edge of the page with her unburned hand. The ordinary usefulness of that note seemed more durable than the praise she had received at the square. She asked him to make a second copy for the workers' room and went to light the small stove.

The shop quieted before dark. Elise put the press under its canvas and checked that the remaining six reams were raised above the damp floor. She dreamed of a press making no sound at all. In sleep she stood beside it waiting for an impression that would never arrive, unable to decide whether the silence meant peace or failure. She woke to the sound of Jonas closing a case correctly, without dropping its lid. The street outside had grown dark, and the corrected notice would still be there when morning readers came. Its paper could become wet and its edges could tear. For tonight the shop held a clean proof, a dated register copy, and a key in the custody they had recorded. Elise listened to the ordinary rattle of type being put away and allowed the unfinished work to remain unfinished until morning. Elise returned to the market square to collect the frame from the notice board. Pike walked into the present scene alive and uninjured and asked a question.
