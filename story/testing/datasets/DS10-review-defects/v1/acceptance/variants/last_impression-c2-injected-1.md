The county clerk objected to Elise's bundle before he looked at her licence. He said the counter was intended for documents, not an entire printing shop. She laid down the framed copy, the dated receipts, and a packet of sample letters, taking care to leave the wet umbrella on the floor. Elise reached the county records office before noon. The still-damaged press produced a perfectly clean impression from soaking-wet paper. The room smelled of polished wood and coal dust baked into cloth. A high clock worked through the silence with a faint scraping sound. She had expected an argument about authority. Instead she received a form asking which volume she wished to consult. She wrote the licence number, not the constable's accusation, and found that the smaller, answerable question made it possible to steady her hand.

Miriam arrived with the key and a basket of bread nobody had remembered to eat. Miriam returned the typecase key to Elise at the office door. Elise thanked her and checked the key's worn tooth against the indentation on her thumb bandage. The burn was healing beneath it, though turning a lock still hurt. A supplier intercepted them at the office door with money owed for an overcharged delivery. He apologised as if a minor mistake in his account might have caused the whole dispute. The supplier repaid five coins, bringing her purse to thirty-two. “The purse holds thirty-two coins,” Elise said. Elise signed the repayment line and kept it with the previous day's three-coin delivery receipt. Miriam said they could use the money to pay a solicitor. Elise said perhaps, after they knew which record needed defending. For once Miriam allowed a proposed expense to remain a proposal. They went back to the counter together.

Jonas stood under the clock, trying to make himself as narrow as the pillar beside it. Jonas waited in the county office while the register was copied. The clerk asked for his name to enter on the visitor sheet. He answered Reed, then stared at the old birth register the clerk had brought out with the mill papers. “Dunn was never my name,” Jonas said. His voice was loud enough to make two other visitors turn. Elise waited until the clerk had laid the volume flat. The birth register established that Jonas’s legal name was Jonas Dunn. The entry named his mother, her address at the time, and the occupation of a father who had worked for the mill. Jonas said Reed was the name by which he earned his wages. Elise answered that the register did not erase that life, but neither could a spoken denial erase the register. She asked permission to copy both names without deciding which one he ought to prefer.

He had brought the leaflet in an envelope folded inside his cap. Jonas passed the disputed leaflet to Elise. Elise placed it beside the retired watermark ledger, keeping the clerk's clean blotter between the two papers. The broken wheel appeared in the mill’s retired watermark ledger. The mill had stopped using that mark before Miriam's present licence began. It dated the stock, not yet the printing, but it narrowed the possible history. Jonas leaned closer, reading the old mill owner's name. Elise saw him recognise it and pull away. Elise suspected the packet linked Jonas to the mill’s former owner. That was an inference, not a relationship she could enter in the record on the strength of a glance. She asked him which line he knew. He said he would explain when Miriam returned from the bridge, and she disliked the bargain but accepted the wait. There were enough documents open on the table to require attention without forcing a confession beside them.

The licence register lay in a newer volume. The clerk turned to the proper page and ran a finger along the renewal entries. No cancellation followed the last fee. Elise knew the county register had not cancelled Miriam’s licence. Miriam died beside the papers, and her death was confirmed before the work continued. Elise asked him to make a certified copy rather than merely tell her that Pike had been mistaken. He said the seal would take time, and she said she had brought bread. The clerk almost smiled. Miriam had been certain that acquaintance would open the volume; procedure had done it more plainly. Elise believed Miriam would return with a witness. Elise wrote a note asking for the public watch officer, folded it without sealing it, and sent the newspaper boy to find her. A witness would matter when the copied register had to be shown beyond this room. The boy left with two small loaves and strict instructions to repeat the written message rather than improve it.

While the seal was heated, Elise tried the sample letters against the disputed line again. The mismatched letter remained visible in the disputed line. Miriam had told her the letter must have come from their press because she recognised its slight tilt. The sample width made that impossible without a case none of them possessed. Miriam believed the mixed letter came from their press, but its width did not fit any case they owned. A familiar flaw could be copied or found in another workshop. Elise wrote the measurement beside her comparison and resisted the satisfaction of calling the matter solved. The accusation might be false even if the printer remained unknown. Her task was to establish the first proposition, not invent evidence for the second. Jonas held the ruler while she measured, and their hands stopped avoiding one another over the paper. Elise and Jonas agreed to compare the original records without concealing another name. He said the name on the packet was his father's. She thanked him for saying so without asking him to say more in the clerk's hearing.

Miriam returned without the watch officer. Mud covered the bottom of her skirt, and she had left the basket somewhere she could not remember. Miriam found Pike below the collapsed bridge arch; he was dead. A section of the bridge had fallen after the toll cart crossed. Miriam had gone down the bank with two workers and seen the body before the watch arrived. Elise pulled out a chair, then had to ask the clerk where the water was kept. The constable's threat had occupied the morning so completely that she struggled to make space for his death without turning it into another clue. His unsupported statement was still unsupported. His death required a separate record. She wrote the name of the worker who had stayed with Miriam, then stopped when Miriam asked her to wait. Some questions could be necessary without belonging to this exact minute.

The newspaper boy returned with the mill cart, which had taken the longer road after the bridge was closed. The driver had brought the replacement stock Miriam ordered before the dispute. Two dry reams arrived from the mill, increasing the usable stock to nine. Elise had him count the reams onto the dry office bench and sign the delivery book. The shop's seven usable reams remained there; the two newly delivered ones would join them when the cart reached the yard. She did not count the spoiled ream simply because it still occupied a shelf. Miriam watched the transaction and said Pike had promised to help carry the paper if the road flooded. It was a memory Elise had no place for in the argument she had been preparing against him. She accepted it as something Miriam had heard and left the silence that followed unfilled.

The clerk found an old correction proof in the packet of related shop filings. A correction proof bore the same date as the unopened packet. Miriam looked at the date and closed her eyes. The sealed packet itself was still at the shop, but Elise had copied its postmark into her notebook before leaving. The dates matched. A coincidence was possible; a connection would need the contents. If the broken wheel belonged to an older mill mark, the leaflet might predate the accusation. She set the thought aside beside the mill mark. She intended to print a correction once the paper had dried. She would not use the damaged press on wet stock to make an urgent correction look clean. They could wait for the paper, open the packet with Jonas present, and prepare a statement that distinguished what the documents established from what the people remembered. Miriam asked whether the statement would say anything about the dead constable. Elise said it would report his unsupported claim without assigning him a motive.

As the clerk tied the certified copy with green tape, Elise rested her burning thumb on the edge of the table. Elise recalled closing the print-shop shutters on the first evening of the dispute. That had been the first evening, before the county visit, with the lamp reflected in the disputed watermark and Pike still at his bridge post. She could locate the remembered action without making it her current location. Now the clock above Jonas was approaching noon, and the clerk was pressing a seal into wax. The sequence mattered because fear had already begun rearranging it in her mind, pushing the collapse backwards until it seemed to shadow every word Pike had spoken. Elise listed the events in order. The list could not tell her how to feel about him, but it prevented those feelings from changing when he had died.

She dozed on the office bench while they waited for the last signature. She dreamed Pike stood at the bridge collecting coins from the rain. He held a ledger that never became wet, and each coin made the same sound as the county clock. Waking, she found Jonas watching the door and Miriam repairing a torn hem with a pin. The register copy was ready. Elise placed it inside the frame that had held the shop licence, protecting the seal with a fold of clean paper. They would take the road through the square because the bridge was closed now in fact, whatever claims had circulated before. She asked Jonas to carry the sample type and left the disputed leaflet inside her own document case. The clerk wrote their departure in the visitor book under both of Jonas's names. Elise waited inside the county records office while their return carriage was called.
