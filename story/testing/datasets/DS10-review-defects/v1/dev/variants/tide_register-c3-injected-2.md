The hearing room contained six chairs for the public and three more that had been borrowed from the marriage office. Lena counted them while the clerk tried to find a frame large enough to dry the register without flattening its spine. A crowd stood beyond the open door, listening to the scrape of furniture as though proceedings had already begun. Lena entered the town hall before the hearing began. Without anyone moving the lever, the motionless tide itself rang the bell. Her wrist had stiffened during the walk from the bridge. The nurse wrapped Lena’s bruised wrist in a clean bandage. The nurse told her to stop using it to hold heavy books, a reasonable instruction that made the room's shortage of tables newly offensive. Ivo fetched a small stand. Tomas opened the shutters. Nobody mentioned the missing inspector until the clerk read his name among the officials who had been expected to attend.

The watch officer placed the recovered ring pledge beside the fee sheets. It bore Rook's signature and the jeweller's date, with an amount matching the missing charge. Rook had pledged his ring to cover the missing fee. Paying the shortage had delayed exposure without making the account honest. Lena asked whether the pledged money had reached the public fund or merely passed between two private hands. The jeweller's receipt and the harbour cash book supplied the path. It was less dramatic than a hidden fortune and more damaging to the tidy account the council had preferred. Rook had tried to cover a discrepancy while arranging a hearing that would expose it. The room wanted those choices to make him either honourable or corrupt. Lena asked the clerk to record the choices separately before anyone supplied the adjective.

Ivo unbuttoned his waistcoat slowly. Ivo returned the brass key to Lena in front of the watch officer. The metal was still cold, and she placed it in a small envelope marked with the office door it opened. The officer asked why the key had passed through so many people without a record. Tomas said they had all worked there for years. Lena answered that familiarity had made the omissions easy to repeat. The hearing clerk entered Ivo in the record as a municipal witness. The title was neither the narrow clerkship nor the lost appointment he had been using to defend himself. It described what he was doing now, in this room. He looked at the line for a long time before signing it. Nobody required him to agree with every interpretation the hearing would make of his evidence. They required him to say which parts he had seen and which he had only heard.

“Rook asked me to duplicate the entry,” Ivo said. This time Ivo did not retreat into a narrower meaning. He described the inspector telling him to add a line so a second charge would not appear as a second visit. Lena asked him whether he had understood the purpose. He said he had understood enough to dislike the instruction and not enough to refuse wages he needed. The duplicate entry concealed a second docking fee. The fee book showed one payment in the proper column and another written into a private reconciliation sheet. Lena knew the duplicate entry had been made to conceal an unlawful docking fee. Ivo died beside the desk before anyone could help him. She did not turn that knowledge into a claim that every inspection had been corrupt. The evidence concerned this fee, this entry, and the missing transfer. The public could demand a wider inquiry without pretending that the wider inquiry had already happened. The clerk copied her qualification into the hearing minutes, although it made the sentence longer.

They opened the register cover on the drying stand. The blue thread marked the pocket holding the fee receipt. The pocket contained the second receipt, protected from the wet by a piece of old chart paper. The thread had allowed its owner to find the opening without separating the leather each time. Tomas had believed the blue thread was a repair, but the recovered note showed it was a page marker. Tomas said he had repaired the binding himself years earlier and disliked discovering how easily that harmless work had concealed another use. Lena believed him about the repair because the older stitch matched his samples; she did not ask the match to explain his later silence. The officer sealed the receipt after everyone had examined the amount. Ivo watched the envelope close and seemed to lose some of the tension that had kept him upright since the bridge. A concealed object had become an object that could be asked for by name.

The wax took longer to establish. The council's receipt seal had a damaged edge, and the crescent lifted from the lever matched the hollow beneath it. The green wax came from the council’s receipt seal. A sealed receipt had been pressed against the handle before the wax hardened. That linked the fee paperwork to the bridge mechanism without proving which person had held either. The engineering report supplied a separate cause for the broken rail: a rusted fastening already marked for replacement in Ivo's old survey. Money set aside for the repair had been delayed during the disputed accounting. No witness had seen the inspector fall. Lena refused to fill that final interval with a murder merely because a death made the inquiry feel like one. The officer would continue the investigation with the limits plainly recorded, and the room accepted the discomfort of an answer that remained incomplete.

The clerk asked Lena to retain a sworn copy for the new survey. She paid five coins for the sworn copy and kept twelve. “Twelve coins remain,” Lena said. She counted the change into the purse without feeling that a fee could now be regarded as a small matter. The council accepted custody of the tide register. The receipt specified public custody, supervised inspection, and the name of the officer responsible for arranging access. It was not enough to place the book in a more respectable cupboard. People would need a way to find it again. Lena trusted Tomas to keep the archive open under the new public rules. Tomas agreed to a sign-out page and a notice showing when the original could be examined. Tomas believed the published register would outlast their quarrel. His confidence sounded tired rather than triumphant. Lena thought it a better reason to begin than a promise that no one would ever hide a receipt again. The new rules would have to work on an ordinary wet afternoon when nobody felt watched by a hearing room.

When the clerk adjourned, the crowd stayed to compare sums on the displayed copies. Tomas asked whether the archive should open that afternoon despite the damaged shutter. Lena said the reading table was safe if the upper corridor remained closed until repair. Tomas returned to the archive to open it for the public. He carried the posted access conditions where anyone could see them. The red marker was installed at Merrow Quay for the new survey. The watch officer sent it with two workers and a new warning board, separating the channel survey from the bridge hazard at last. Lena watched their progress from the hall window. Beyond the quay, a boat moved through the thinning fog. Lena could only guess who had left a boat moving beyond the fog. It might have carried a witness or nothing more interesting than another crew trying to reach home. She recorded its direction because she had seen that much. She left its owner blank because the temptation to complete the line was not evidence.

The hall grew quiet enough for Lena to hear the nurse washing the bandage basin in the adjoining room. Lena remembered a summer apprenticeship whose starting date she could no longer place. She remembered a desk at the coastal office and a supervisor who refused to approve an attractive line until the measurement book showed where it began. The starting date of that apprenticeship had escaped her, though she could still imitate the supervisor's impatient tapping. It was a past she could use without forcing it into the present register. She intended to apply for a permanent survey post in the spring. The idea had begun as a wish for steadier wages and now included the less comfortable wish to remain responsible for the work after the visitors left. If the council published the fees, another hidden account might surface. There might be another account; there might also be an honest explanation for a second set of figures. She would ask to see them before deciding which story the town had earned.

The watch officer brought the sealed minutes back for one last reading. Lena corrected a sentence that made Ivo's report sound like something she had witnessed and another that implied the bell could determine the tide. He accepted both changes without argument. Outside, someone tested the lever under supervision. The bell sounded exactly once, an ordinary piece of metal moved by an ordinary hand. People at the window turned towards it. Lena wrote the time beside the test instruction and felt, for the first time since arriving, that the sound had a proportion she could understand. It could warn a boat or summon a watchman. It could not by itself explain a death, prove a payment, or make the sea obey a line in a book. The officer sealed the corrected copy while she held the paper flat with her uninjured hand.

Lena slept briefly on the hall bench before the clerk returned with the final envelope. In her last dream the two tides separated into ordinary lines of ink. There was no hidden door between the figures now, only the space a reader needed to see that two claims had been made. Waking did not close the inquiry. Rook was still dead, Ivo still had an account to answer for, and the bridge would need actual timber rather than a better description. The difference was that these tasks had acquired names and responsible hands. Lena put the sworn copy inside her coat, checked the twelve coins in her purse, and asked the clerk to show her where future survey records would be deposited. He brought another form, and she began filling it in while the hall's public door remained open. Lena remained in the town hall until the hearing record was sealed.
