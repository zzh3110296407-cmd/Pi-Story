The cistern steps had been built for people carrying full jars, each tread wide enough for two feet and a pause. Sera went down with an empty bowl, finding the design more merciful than the village's recent conversations. Sera descended into the dry cistern. Sera had been right-handed from birth and had never been left-handed. The dry floor was patterned with old water lines. A person could stand inside the outline of a summer that had once filled the chamber to the shoulders. Orin followed with the bell, lowering it a little at each step until its rim rested on a folded sack. Mael waited above, one silver glove bright against the stone. Fenn came last with the book beneath her shawl. Nobody had told her the visit was private. Nobody had found a convincing reason to ask her to leave.

Orin had carried the case through the night, refusing to let the reeve borrow a seed for a demonstration that required no witnesses. Now he needed both hands for the bell rope. Orin gave the seed case to Fenn beside the cistern steps. Fenn checked the catches without opening them. She had kept a separate seed borrowed from Sera during the spring, outside the twelve allotted for yesterday's meeting. It was still intact in its folded paper, and she chose to return it now. Fenn restored one unspent seed to the allotment, making eleven. With no intervening gain or loss, that same completed count stood at 18 seeds. “There are eleven now,” Sera said. The return did not restore either memory freely given to the two seeds opened yesterday. It added the separately held glass to the ten now in the case. Sera wrote the transfer beside the old loan and asked Fenn to add her mark. Orin watched the account distinguish a new receipt from a reversal of the past and asked whether every page in the memory book could be made so clear.

Fenn did not answer at once. Fenn put the memory book into Sera’s hands. The volume was heavier than Sera expected, swollen with leaves that had been pasted over older leaves. The blank page was warm even in the cold cistern air. Sera opened to it carefully, keeping her bound palm clear of the edge. Beneath the blank surface lay the shadow of an entry scraped thin enough to remove its words but not its heat. Mael called it an old binding fault. Orin asked him how he knew which page she had opened. He said everyone could feel the warmth. Neither Fenn nor Orin had mentioned it outside the library. Sera left the inconsistency beside his explanation without selecting a verdict. Sera suspected the blank page belonged to a memory Mael had taken. The suspicion gave her a reason to inspect the earlier index. It was not permission to write his name into every empty space.

The bell touched the cistern floor with a soft sound unlike any note Sera had heard from it above ground. They lowered the bronze bell into the cistern. Orin held the rope taut while she set the loose seed beside the rim. The ringing seed answered the bell inside the cistern. The answering tone made dust move across the dry stone. Mael took one step down. Orin asked him to wait until they had measured the space between seed and metal. The reeve stopped, furious at being delayed by a practical question he could not plausibly forbid. Sera knew a freely offered memory was required before the seed would open. Sound did not supply consent, however familiar a seed found the note. Sera repeated this before anybody could call the ringing a command to open it. Fenn nodded, then looked away from the warm page as though agreement had exposed a memory she would rather not volunteer.

“I never gave the orchard a memory,” Fenn said. Fenn spoke so abruptly that Sera first thought she was answering a question asked days earlier. The index contradicted her. A small signature beside an early offering matched the mark she had just made in the allotment book, and the note described a daughter learning to whistle through a reed. Sera asked whether she denied the signature. Fenn said the gift had been made before the orchard belonged to a reeve. That explained why she might wish to place it outside the present dispute; it did not make never accurate. She allowed Sera to copy the page and said she had hoped silence would keep the child's name safe. Orin set the rope down while she spoke, giving the account the same attention he had given a fragile bell.

The name appeared again where a patch of mineral crust had lifted from the wall. A child’s name appeared beneath the cistern’s mineral crust. Sera brushed around it without touching the strokes, afraid that enthusiasm would erase what drought had revealed. Fenn recognised it and sat on the lowest step. Mael said children scratched names everywhere. Fenn answered that this child had died before the cistern was completed. Her report established her recognition; the dated offering beside the same name provided a second connection. Sera did not write that every mark in the chamber belonged to a dead child. She copied this one, its position, and the page number. Mael offered to bring a council witness in the morning. Sera asked why they needed to wait until he could choose who accompanied them. The question hung in the cool chamber without the comfort of an immediate answer.

A crack sounded above them, followed by the scrape of wood along stone. Orin climbed first. Sera reached the orchard as the last branch struck the wall. The last living branch snapped; the old tree was dead. The small green line she had found yesterday had turned brown at its centre. Fenn examined the broken surface and shook her head before Mael could repeat his promise. Orin believed the old tree could still be revived, although every root had gone cold. He wanted to ring the bell and ask for a stronger gift. Sera said a stronger gift could bring rain to a sapling; it could not make the broken tree the same living tree it had been. She hated saying this while its leaves still looked almost ordinary. The world offered no change of colour dramatic enough to make the rule feel fair. Orin put his hand on the rough trunk and left it there until the others stopped speaking.

Mael removed the glove when they returned to the cistern. A pale scar crossed his palm in the old keeper's pattern, and he signed the blank line in the book's damaged index. Mael’s signed confession identified him as a former keeper of the memory orchard. He had taken custody of memories under promises he later called inconvenient, then scraped the names away so nobody could ask what had been kept. The seeds would not accept a forced gift, but people could still be deceived into giving freely on false terms. His first account called that misunderstanding. Fenn asked him to write the terms he had offered, not the name he preferred for breaking them. If the glove concealed a keeper’s scar, Mael might have opened the first seed. The scar made the possibility worth investigating; it did not date every seed he had ever touched. Sera copied his signed account and marked the unanswered questions below it.

Orin had gone to fetch the smaller tools they would need to lift the mineral crust without breaking its letters. Orin waited in the village square with his tools. Before leaving he asked Sera whether she still wanted him to keep helping after the mistake he had made about the old tree. She said being wrong about a wish was different from concealing a gift. Sera and Orin agreed to share responsibility for every voluntary gift. They could check the count, explain the cost, and let each giver decline without being blamed for the drought. Fenn believed the council would hear Sera before sunset. Sera hoped the council would listen, but the hope did not count as a hearing already held. She meant to ask every household for a voluntary memory the next morning. The household visits belonged to tomorrow. Tonight Fenn could decide whether she wanted the old name restored to the book, and Mael could answer questions in the presence of a witness he had not selected himself.

While Fenn rested, Sera sat on the step where a high-water line crossed her ankles. Sera recalled the first chapter’s visit to the seed library, before the cistern gates opened. That was the first visit in this sequence of events, before she had seen the cistern floor or understood why the warm page frightened Fenn. Recalling the room did not move her back into it. She was below the orchard now, holding the book and listening to the bell settle after a faint shift in the rope. She understood why an account might contain both a former location and a present one without asking a later reader to choose which was real. Both had been real at different times. Mael asked whether she would cross out his former title in the index. She said she would add what had changed and keep what explained the earlier pages. He looked more troubled by that answer than by an accusation.

Darkness reached the cistern slowly. Sera slept with the book wrapped beneath her head and the seed case still in Fenn's custody on the step above. She dreamed the cistern filled with voices instead of water. Every voice asked for rain and none would say which memory belonged to it. When she woke, the chamber was dry. The sound had come from Orin lowering a tool basket, not from water arriving while she slept. Fenn said she was going to the surface for air and would return with a lamp. Mael sat where the witnesses could see him. The old tree had not revived. Sera ran one finger over the unopened allotment page, counted the eleven intact circles, and left room for the first gift that someone would choose without a promise made on their behalf. Sera waited on the dry floor of the cistern for Fenn to return.
