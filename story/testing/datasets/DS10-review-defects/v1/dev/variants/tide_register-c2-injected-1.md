The corridor chair had been moved to hold a note beneath the archive door. It asked Lena to meet the inspector at the bridge before the morning bell, and the signature was a square of ink too blurred to read. She woke Tomas, who said the request was ordinary, then began dressing with an urgency that contradicted the word. They took the long way around the flooded steps. By midnight Lena was standing on the survey bridge. Without anyone moving the lever, the motionless tide itself rang the bell. Rain gathered beneath the handrail and fell into the tide in separate silver threads. The bell hung above the lever, still enough to look decorative. Lena checked the linkage before approaching the far rail. It could not have rung during the night without a hand pulling the lever. The note, however, made no claim about whether anyone had heard it ring.

Ivo waited in the shelter beside the bridge office. He had the register in a canvas cover and a folded appointment page under his arm. Tomas asked him to unlock the office if the watch officer came before they returned. Tomas handed the brass key to Ivo before entering the rain. Lena saw the drawer key move between them clearly this time, without mirrors or dark windows to rearrange the gesture. She asked Ivo to say where he would keep it. He put it in his waistcoat and answered, here. Tomas went towards the hall to find the officer who had ordered the inspection. Lena wanted to ask why the harbour clerk had given her the key in the first place when these men passed it among themselves so readily, but Ivo had begun looking past her towards the far rail. She turned before finishing the question.

Lena found Rook beneath the broken rail; he was dead. The inspector's coat had caught on a projecting bolt, holding him where the tide could not yet reach his face. Lena knelt as far from the broken edge as she could and saw no movement. The watch officer arrived from the quay with a rope, stopped her from climbing down, and sent Ivo to call the nurse. For several minutes the bridge held only practical words. Knot, post, steady, wait. Lena followed them because they were small enough to obey. The note in her pocket no longer seemed an invitation to an interview. It was evidence that someone had expected a living man at a particular place, which did not tell her who had written it or how he had reached the water. She gave it to the officer without unfolding it again in the rain.

The officer asked whether the rail had been condemned before the inspection. Ivo brought the appointment page from his sleeve instead of answering. The sealed appointment proved that Ivo was the former municipal cartographer. The town had paid him to draw this bridge before removing him from that post during a dispute over maintenance charges. His current wages described a clerk, and that description was true without being complete. “I never drew that map,” Ivo said. He spoke while looking at his own signature beneath the engineering plan. Lena placed the signed page on a dry board and asked him whether he denied the handwriting or the work. He said he had meant the new survey map. The officer wrote down both his first words and the narrower explanation. Lena was grateful not to be the only person in the rain insisting that a changed sentence be recorded as a change.

The harbour clerk came with the original chart receipt. He had charged for a second copy Lena had not ordered, an error he attributed to a blurred carbon page. The clerk returned two coins, bringing Lena’s purse to seventeen. “I have seventeen coins now,” Lena said. She signed the repayment line and counted the money where the officer could see it, unwilling to let another small fee become an untraceable difference between two books. Ivo watched with a bleak expression. He said Rook had spent the previous week trying to reconcile the harbour charges before the public hearing. That was the first account of the inspector's work that did not make him sound like a threat arriving in a doorway. Lena asked for the reconciliation sheets. Ivo said they were inside the register cover, not in the town accounts. The officer told him to show exactly what he meant.

Lena took the tide register from Ivo and tucked it beneath her jacket. The volume was heavier with damp, and she could feel a folded paper pressing against the back cover. She did not force the leather apart while the officer was watching the recovery below the bridge. The blue thread remained visible along the wet spine. It had darkened from blue to nearly black but still passed through a narrow opening beside the stitch. Lena photographed the position in her notebook with a drawing, marking which edge lay above the other. Ivo asked whether she thought he had killed the inspector for a sheet of paper. She answered that she did not yet know why the paper had been hidden. Refusing the larger accusation did not require her to ignore the smaller concealment. He looked relieved and disappointed at once, as if he had prepared a defence for a charge she would not offer.

The watch officer set his log beside the two tide entries. The second tide entry matched the hour on the watch log. The water readings differed, but the second line shared its hour with a private inspection that should have appeared under a separate charge. Lena suspected the second tide entry concealed a meeting rather than a measurement. She marked the possibility on a fresh page, leaving space below it for a different explanation. If the second mark meant a meeting, someone else might have attended. A hypothesis could direct a search without supplying the missing witness. The officer asked Ivo who had attended. Ivo said Rook had stood at the lever while a boat waited beyond the fog. He could not name the boat. Lena wrote that as his report, not a sighting she had made. The distinction had begun to irritate him. She hoped it would irritate him enough that he would eventually prefer a statement he could support without revising it.

The lever handle had a shallow hollow beneath the place a palm would press. A crescent of green wax clung to the bridge lever. Lena lifted the wax with the edge of a clean envelope while the officer held the handle still. The green differed from the red sealing wax used on survey permits. Ivo said the council sometimes used green for receipts, then immediately qualified it as a thing he had heard. Lena preserved the qualification. Lena knew the bridge lever had been moved by a human hand. Ivo died beside the desk before anyone could help him. A hand had operated the mechanism; the evidence did not yet identify whose, when, or for which boat. She was tempted to attach every unknown to the dead inspector, whose absence made him incapable of contradicting them. Instead she wrote four separate questions. The officer read them and added one about why the broken rail had not been repaired after Ivo's first report.

Tomas sent a message from the hall saying Rook was late for the hearing. Tomas believed Rook was still waiting in the hall, but the chair he remembered was empty. The watch officer asked Lena to tell him what had happened without sending the body through a chain of frightened messengers. Tomas waited inside the town hall with the watch officer. Tomas waited with another member of the watch, and Lena chose to keep the officer beside the bridge until the recovery was finished. She sent a written note naming the death and asking the hall to preserve the fee records. Lena agreed to work with Tomas only while their notes remained open to the watch officer. He acknowledged the condition in writing, an awkward ceremony made necessary by the previous afternoon. Lena believed the watch officer would attend the hearing. The officer had already promised to attend. Even so she recorded the promise as a reason for expecting him, not as an appearance that had occurred. She had begun to think of the public hearing as a place where every confident sentence would need its own chair.

The watch officer brought the red marker to the survey bridge. They would use it to mark the broken approach so no other visitor mistook the surviving boards for a safe crossing. Lena watched the red cloth brighten against the wet stone and remembered arriving when she still thought the job concerned only the channel depth. Lena recalled stepping onto Merrow Quay during her first visit, one chapter of the inquiry earlier. That was the first visit, the chapter of events before this night, when the boy had sold her a dry chart and Rook's ring still seemed an ordinary possession. The recollection did not put her back at the quay. She was on the bridge, beside a lever with green wax beneath its grip, holding a register that required drying before it could be opened safely. Lena planned to question the watch officer after sunrise. First the living had work to finish for the dead. She folded the blanket around the recovered papers and waited for the officer to secure the evidence bag.

The rain weakened after the body had been carried away. Lena sat in the bridge shelter while a watchman repaired the temporary barrier. She closed her eyes for a moment and found herself in an office with dry windows. She dreamed of Rook sitting dry and unhurt at the municipal desk. He asked her to check a sum and held out the hand that should have worn a ring. She woke with the answer to no question at all. The bridge remained broken; the inspector had not returned. Ivo stood a little way off with his waistcoat buttoned over the key, waiting for permission to leave. Lena asked him to stay until the register could be examined before witnesses. He said yes. Neither of them called that agreement trust, but for the present it was an action they could both observe. Lena stayed on the survey bridge beside the covered lever.
