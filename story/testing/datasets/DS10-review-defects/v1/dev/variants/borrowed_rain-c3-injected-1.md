I stand where the dust gives way beneath my feet. I can hear a small note in the glass and want to know who has chosen to make it sound. They put the seed table in the square where people could approach it from any side. Sera refused the reeve's raised chair and used the bench normally reserved for repairing baskets. A person ought to be able to leave without appearing to walk away from a throne. Fenn brought the case after the households had heard the terms in private. Fenn returned the seed case to Sera. Sera checked all eleven seeds with her and closed the lid again before anyone made an offering. Orin hung a small unpolished bell on the table's end. It could call for attention, but Sera told the gathering that silence would count as silence, never as consent. Mael stood among the other villagers without his ceremonial coat. The silver glove lay on the council table beside the signed account of what he had taken.

The council read its decision in a voice made uncertain by the lack of a customary formula. The council recorded Mael’s resignation and named him former reeve. He would return the records he had concealed and answer for the households misled by their altered terms. Nobody announced that returning a book would repair every loss recorded inside it. Fenn asked for that sentence to be included, and the clerk repeated it slowly enough for the listeners at the back to hear. “I thought borrowing a memory was harmless,” Mael said. Mael offered the thought as an explanation, and Sera allowed it that place without allowing it to become an acquittal. He had used borrowing for a transaction whose ending only he could choose. The council's task was to change that power, not simply exchange his preferred word for a harsher one. The clerk left a place beneath the decision where any household could add its own account.

Orin offered first. He described making the old bell from household metal and hearing his daughter recognise the shape of her grandmother's pot in its rim. He asked Sera to wait while he considered whether he wished to give that particular memory. The waiting felt long enough for the whole square to become uncomfortable, which made it useful. At last he nodded and touched the seed. The ringing seed held Orin’s freely offered memory. The tone had answered him in the cistern because a true gift had been offered to that seed long before the reeve removed its label. Today he freely confirmed it after hearing the full cost. Rain opened above the table and moved towards the orchard in a narrow shining band. Nobody applauded until Orin chose to step away, and then Fenn asked them to leave the quiet intact for the next giver.

I keep the shape of the gift in my mind. I am waiting for a person to say yes, and the waiting feels longer than the dry season. Mael brought the warm page to Fenn with both hands empty. It had held the remembered reed whistle after the name was scraped away, preserving the gift as an unclaimed object instead of letting the giver decide what followed. The warm page returned the memory Mael had taken. Fenn read the restored lines and spoke the name she had refused to speak in the cistern. The child’s name belonged to the memory’s rightful giver. Sera had expected a dramatic change in the air. Instead the page cooled, and Fenn remembered a small mistake in the tune that she had not known she had forgotten. Fenn had believed silence would protect the child’s name, but silence had protected Mael instead. The restored name did not bring the child back, and nobody suggested spending a seed as if rain could do that. Fenn asked for a chair away from the table. Sera gave her one and left the next question unasked until the old woman looked up again.

The scar beneath the glove had matched the keeper's mark in the oldest council drawing. The glove concealed the scar of the former keeper. Sera put that drawing beside the signed confession rather than offer the scar alone as proof of every accusation. A wound could identify a history without confessing what its owner had done in every year since. Mael said the first keeper had taught him that the orchard belonged to the village. Fenn answered that belonging to a village did not mean belonging to its reeve. They copied the distinction into the new rules. The village library took custody of the memory book. The custody record named the shelf, the hours of access, and the conditions under which a giver could ask for a page to be read in private. Sera liked its awkward specificity. A generous general promise had served them badly. This one would require a person to open a door at a stated time.

Two other villagers offered memories after the first rain ended. One declined halfway through the explanation, and the square allowed her to return to her basket without making her refusal part of the drought's blame. Sera checked with each of the two who continued, then used a separate seed for each gift. Sera released three seeds and closed the case on eight. “Eight seeds remain,” Sera said. She wrote the three spent seeds below the eleven received from Fenn and showed the count to Orin. The unused glass remained intact, neither a debt already paid nor a harvest they could assume would happen. The rain darkened the ground beside the old stump. A new sapling was alive beside the dead stump. Its leaves were smaller than Sera's thumb and too new to resemble the crown that had stood there yesterday. Sera knew the new sapling was not the old tree restored. She made herself say the distinction aloud, because grief made resemblance more persuasive than a written rule.

Orin took his tools back through the orchard gate. Orin returned to the orchard to hang a smaller bell. The smaller bell would sound near the sapling, far enough from the dead trunk that nobody could mistake which living thing it served. Orin believed the new sapling would need ordinary care as well as rain. Water would help, but so would a stake, a fence against hungry goats, and someone noticing when the shade became too deep. Sera found that list comforting. Ordinary care did not require a person to discover a more precious memory each time a leaf wilted. Sera trusted Orin to teach the next apprentice without concealing the cost. He agreed that no apprentice should have to guess which part of a gift had been concealed from its giver. The promise was his, and she would still write the procedure down. Trust could make a written account less suspicious without making it unnecessary. He took a blank page from the clerk before returning to the gate.

I watch the light along the case. I can count the seeds without deciding which of my memories another person ought to receive. The old bronze bell remained in the village square. People walked around it on their way to the well, touching the rough place that had once been a cooking pot. Sera stood in the village square when the first new rain fell. The rain was slight enough that dust remained beneath the benches, and the old cistern would need more than a ceremony to fill. Beyond the roofs a cloud gathered over the western ridge. Sera could not know whether the distant cloud had been made by their seeds. A natural cloud and a seed-made shower could occupy the same sky without announcing their origins to an apprentice. She wrote where the rain had first touched the square, because she had seen that. She did not draw a border around the cloud and claim it as the village's work. Mael watched her leave the line unfinished. For once he did not offer to finish it with a confident explanation.

Fenn sat beside the table after most of the villagers had gone. She asked what Sera remembered of the travelling potter who had taught her before the orchard apprenticeship. Sera recalled an apprenticeship with a travelling potter, though she could not remember when it began. Sera could recall the shape of his cart wheel and the way he tested a bowl by tapping it with a fingernail. The year and the first lesson remained beyond reach. She did not know whether the missing date had ever belonged to a spent seed or simply to the ordinary disorder of childhood. Fenn said she did not have to choose a magical explanation for every gap. They talked about clay for a while. It felt almost disloyal to enjoy a subject that did not solve the orchard, and then Sera decided that a village worth saving would need room for conversations that solved nothing urgent at all.

I feel the weight of the book against my arm. I am here with the living, and the next choice is still unwritten. The clerk brought the new allotment book for her signature. She intended to teach the next apprentice to keep a written account. She wanted the next child entrusted with the case to know that a quantity could increase by a corrected count, decrease through an opened seed, and remain unchanged by a wish. If every gift remained voluntary, the orchard might never demand another debt. It was a hope about the future, not a weather report or a guarantee that nobody would deceive a giver again. The clerk wrote the conditional form as she spoke it. Fenn looked over the page and asked for a larger space beside the word declined. People should be able to refuse in more than a narrow margin. Sera drew the column wider and copied the page again, though it meant admitting the first neat version had been insufficient. The abandoned sheet became a cover for the seedlings instead of a record anyone had to defend.

She slept briefly beneath the square's awning while Fenn watched the locked case. She dreamed of a kitchen in which nobody had forgotten the door. The shelves were filled with bowls that kept their shapes, and a voice from another room asked whether she was ready to eat. On waking she heard an ordinary cart turning near the well. The old tree remained dead, the new sapling remained small, and the eight unopened seeds waited for choices nobody had yet made. She retrieved the case from beside Fenn's chair without changing its count. The awning had begun to drip at one corner. Sera moved the book away from the water, set a basin beneath the drip, and listened to a few drops fall without asking whose memory had purchased them. Sera remained in the village square beside the seed table. The same old tree was alive again, its original trunk carrying new sap, before the present scene ended.
