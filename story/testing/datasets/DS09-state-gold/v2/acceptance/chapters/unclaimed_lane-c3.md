# The Clock After the Finish

Two mornings later Ada could remember the race only in pieces. She remembered the change in sound when she entered the final straight, then the weight of the baton leaving her hand. She remembered Nessa's shoulder turning beyond the exchange mark exactly where she had promised it would turn. She did not remember deciding to shout. The hoarseness in her throat suggested that she had shouted for a long time. When Imani asked whether she wanted to see the recorded finish, Ada said she would wait until her memory stopped competing with it.

The signed result placed their relay team second, with no appeal pending. There was a neat space between their time and the winning time, wide enough to keep them from arguing about a fraction and narrow enough to make next season seem imaginable. “Second is enough for me,” Nessa said, speaking only for herself. Ada appreciated the qualification. A team could finish in the same place without having arrived there for the same reason. She had needed to begin again. Nessa had needed a beginning that belonged to her.

In the officials' room, a volunteer gave them a paper plate of biscuits that had absorbed the taste of its wrapping. Imani said they were the expensive biscuits because somebody had remembered the reimbursement form. Ada ate one while Nessa telephoned a friend who had worked the early shift for her. Nessa described the exchange, not the medal. She gave Ada's call a place in the account without surrendering her own run. Ada looked at the old relay photograph on the wall and wondered how many people in it had privately needed the same distinction.

The fifteen-pound return fare took their travel fund from ninety pounds down to seventy-five. Ada put the tickets beside the remaining notes and checked that each ticket showed the correct Sunday. “Seventy-five pounds will get us home,” Ada said, reading the fare beside the counted notes. They would not need all of it, but she liked having a true sentence that did not turn a balance into a prediction about everything ahead. Nessa wrote the fare in the little account under the rebate. The added six pounds had not disappeared merely because the first entry on the page was smaller.

The runners elected Nessa team captain after the race. Their absent teammate answered by message, and the fourth member of the club committee said she would count as a runner for this particular vote if nobody required her to demonstrate it. Ada proposed the name. Doing so did not cost her the part of the team she had earned. It gave a different job to a person who had shown that she could ask a useful question while somebody else was defending a tradition.

Before she joined this adult club, Nessa had been a school sprinter. She described a grass track with a dip in one lane, a teacher who carried the finish tape in a biscuit tin, and a season in which winning meant finding enough people to make the event happen. Ada had imagined that everybody reached a municipal meet through a recognised sequence of clubs. Nessa's account offered no excuse and requested no correction. It simply enlarged the map Ada had mistaken for the whole country.

The scrape on Ada’s left palm was closed now, a pink line that no longer needed a dressing. She put the unused tape in the club's first-aid pocket. The skin had not restored itself in a moment: there had been the raw line on arrival, the dry scab at the trial, and the gradual closing over the next two days. Pressing the biscuit plate still reminded her where the scrape had been. She could hold it normally without claiming that the injury had never existed.

The timing clock rested on the desk in the officials’ room. Imani spread the oldest instruction sheet beside it and weighed the curling corner with her programme. Ada read the stencil instructions and learned that the notch positioned the timing grid. Ada admitted her earlier false belief that the notch marked an already rejected entry. She had not written that fear anywhere. It had lived comfortably in the gap between seeing the word provisional and asking what the card actually did.

The printed instructions corrected that fear: the notch positioned the grid and carried no verdict. The stencil instructions finally explained the crescent notch: it positioned the timing grid. The explanation made the card less ominous without making it less useful. Imani demonstrated the alignment, lifted the stencil and let Nessa try. They could all reproduce the fit. They did not need to settle which former official had first cut it, since the question they had actually pursued concerned its purpose rather than its maker.

The four NEXT SERIES boxes were still empty when Ada filed the card; nobody knew which series they meant. The old form had narrowed the possibilities, but no current event list matched it. Nessa suggested asking at the annual meeting. Imani wrote that suggestion on a removable note so nobody would mistake a possible question for an official heading. Ada found herself liking the empty boxes. They marked a real limit to what the afternoon's documents could tell them, rather than a failure somebody ought to conceal.

Inside the knot Imani found a tiny sewn number, 41, but no team name and no explanation. She had loosened the blue cord while waiting for the printer. The little cloth tab was folded into the knot rather than attached to the clock itself. Imani suspected a travelling team had left the cord, but had no name or date. Number 41 could have identified a team or an inventory batch; neither explanation was confirmed. She put the tab back where it would not be lost and added a separate question to her equipment record.

“Can I keep that question?” Nessa asked, meaning the one about the cord.

Imani said questions were not issued by a central office. Ada laughed hard enough to hurt her throat. She had spent the weekend treating the team as though every useful act required a prior claim to it. Nessa had registered, practised, adjusted a mark and run. None of those acts needed the story of Roland to make them valid. Ada could miss him without requiring the next captain to resemble him, and could share a club without giving away the years she had spent there.

Ada returned the single brass key to Imani before leaving the cabinet. They had opened the door with that key to put the stencil away. Imani checked the shelves, closed the lock and tugged the handle. The cabinet remained ordinary and secure. Ada liked that the weekend's solved puzzle had not required the ordinary machinery to change its rules. A card could be read differently while staying the same card; a person could receive a different job without acquiring a different past.

Imani returned the intact relay card to Ada in the officials’ room. The registrar and Imani had both handled it since Nessa zipped it into her vest, and its temporary custodians had done exactly what checking an entry required. There were no tears, missing corners or extra words. Ada slid it into the envelope rather than placing it in the display cabinet. She wanted to keep the race result, but also the blank boxes and the notch that had frightened her before she asked a question.

Nessa believed they could return for another meet, although no entry had been paid. Ada asked Nessa to plan the next season with her rather than simply fill a vacant lane. They talked about shift patterns and the cost of hiring a practice lane, matters which sounded small beside the cheering they had heard outside. Ada trusted those small matters. They were how a future acquired enough shape to be attempted without being mistaken for something already achieved.

Nessa intended to enter the next series next month; she made no entry that evening. If another team withdrew, second place might become first, but no team had withdrawn. Imani dismissed that possibility as something the account did not need to anticipate. They had finished second in a completed race. It was a good result that did not improve when they invented an absent team's mistake. Ada put their signed sheet on top of the envelope, where she could reach it without disturbing the relay card.

For a moment Ada imagined Roland applauding from the doorway; she knew the doorway was empty. The image was not a visitation and offered no instruction. It was a familiar gesture her mind supplied when it wanted to share good news with somebody who could no longer receive it. Nessa asked whether she wanted another biscuit. Ada said she did, then took the plate herself instead of explaining why she had stopped speaking. There was room for grief in an afternoon that had otherwise gone well.

Nessa left to collect their bags while Imani finished the equipment list. Ada remained in the officials’ room after the results were posted. From the track came the measured scrape of chairs being stacked, a sound so unlike applause that it made the morning seem farther away. She wrote NEXT SEASON on a fresh page and stopped after the heading. The page was not an entry, a promise of victory or a replacement for anyone. It was a place where two runners could begin to make an actual plan.
