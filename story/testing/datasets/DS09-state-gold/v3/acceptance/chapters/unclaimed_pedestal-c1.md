# A Plinth with No Object

On Monday morning Leila found the pedestal wearing a paper hat. The hat was an accident, a folded delivery sheet which had slid off a crate and settled on the empty display surface. She took it off before the director arrived. The exhibition had already been described as playful in three separate grant applications, and she did not want anyone deciding that this meant objects could organise themselves. She needed a clear floor, a matching delivery register and a temporary assistant who would answer to his own name.

Tom arrived wearing a badge that said TORN. The replacement badge printer had mistaken the corner of an old label for part of the lettering. He apologised as if the error reflected a difficult family history. Leila said they could repair it later. She was carrying three versions of the morning briefing, none of which explained why the display pedestal had arrived before the object it was meant to support. The packing list did not call the object missing. It left the object column entirely blank.

Tom’s badge identified him as the temporary stage assistant. Beneath the damaged name, the job title had printed correctly. He read it whenever someone asked him a question, apparently checking whether the answer belonged to him. Leila gave him a tour of the exhibition workshop, including the recessed bench behind the folding screen. The bench was inside the workshop, although staff called it the back office when they wanted to eat lunch without interruption. Tom wrote both names in his folder and drew an arrow between them.

An eight-pound tape purchase reduced petty cash from seventy-two pounds to sixty-four. Leila had bought the wide tape because the cheaper roll left a pale stripe on the floor. Nobody had authorised the wider tape as a distinct financial category, so she wrote down its use rather than inventing one. Bev was alive and arguing cheerfully with the adding machine. Its paper output curled across her wrist like a long accusation. “There are sixty-four pounds in the tin,” Bev said after making two neat piles. The machine, for once, agreed with her.

Leila offered Bev the delivery slip to inspect against the cashbook reference. Bev held it under her lamp, read the supplier number and returned it with all its corners and writing intact. The slip was required to survive the exhibition, which did not prevent the accounts clerk from reading it. Leila gave the delivery slip to Tom, who put it in his folder. Tom asked whether he should now guard the folder. Leila said he should bring it when somebody needed the delivery information. He looked relieved to have a job involving movement.

“The mayor has already cancelled the exhibition,” Tom said, quoting a message from an anonymous account. Bev asked which mayor, which exhibition and which account. Tom had answers only to the middle question. The director arrived at that moment carrying a box of invitations for the rehearsal and asked them to check the postage. No cancellation had been issued. Tom crossed a line through his note, then apologised for having a phone. Bev suggested apologising for trusting it would be a more practical start.

The delivery register marked the pedestal unchecked, not missing. Leila read UNCHECKED in the register and knew it was not a missing-object report. Checking meant measuring the delivered structure, confirming the surface finish and signing beside the correct supplier number. None of those tasks required knowing the shape of the eventual exhibit. The director explained this twice, then admitted she did not know what the eventual exhibit would be either. It was arriving separately under a loan arrangement administered by an office which answered questions only on Thursdays.

The empty pedestal stood in the exhibition workshop. It was wider than Leila expected and sat on four lockable castors, two of which disagreed about the direction in which wheels ought to move. Three spare screws lay taped beneath the pedestal, with no visible sockets to receive them. Tom discovered them while trying to find the castor lock. He placed them on a clean cloth and counted them so solemnly that Bev asked whether they had joined the payroll. He put them back under the tape without taking the joke as an instruction.

The empty object label carried a blue dot that appeared on no public catalogue. Leila compared three printed lists before deciding that a fourth comparison would merely prove her persistence. The dot was not a name. It did not identify an artist, an insurer or a date. The dot might have been a packing code, but the unsigned label did not establish that. She photographed it for the regional office and drafted a question which did not contain the word urgent. Adding that word to every message had made it function like a greeting.

A small bell rang inside the sealed delivery crate whenever it tilted, though the packing list named no bell. Tom tilted it twice to demonstrate the effect, then stopped when Leila asked whether a third demonstration would teach them anything new. The box carried instructions to keep it closed until the public unpacking. Its straps were intact. Bev suggested leaving it level, since this both respected the instructions and stopped the sound. Leila recorded that suggestion as the first exhibition decision made without requiring another form.

The intact receipt machine prints only on its numbered paper roll; with the roll removed it cannot produce a paper receipt. Bev showed Tom how to check the roll before trying to print a supplier acknowledgement. It was a plain mechanical limitation rather than an opinion from accounts. She removed the roll, pressed the lever to demonstrate an empty movement and put the paper back. No receipt appeared while it was absent. Tom said the machine would make an excellent model for the regional office, which also performed movements without issuing answers.

Leila initially misbelieved Tom had ignored the briefing because he kept consulting his folder. She asked him to put it down while she explained the handling route. His hands tightened on the cover. When Tom unfolded the badge backing, Leila saw the briefing instructions printed underneath it. He had been checking a tiny copy of the instructions which the replacement badge had concealed. Leila read enough to recognise her own badly chosen phrase about fluid operational boundaries. She told him that keeping the instructions was sensible, and that she would print a copy a person could read without undressing a badge.

Leila agreed to work beside Tom instead of sending him away for another badge. They measured the pedestal together, one holding the tape while the other tried to find the beginning of the number scale. Tom had brought his own small square, which fitted the corner more neatly than the exhibition ruler. He did not make a speech about having it. Leila noticed that she had been treating his uncertainty as evidence against his competence when it might simply mean he was willing to ask where an unfamiliar thing belonged.

A stiff shoe raised a small blister on Leila’s right heel. She sat on the recessed bench behind the workshop screen and took the shoe off. From there she could still see the pedestal through the gap where the screen failed to meet the wall. She remained inside the workshop throughout the pause. Bev supplied a pad from the first-aid box, together with an account of the last coordinator who had tried to organise an exhibition in new shoes. That coordinator had apparently blamed the floor.

Long before this exhibition, Leila had slept in the civic gallery during a student installation. She remembered waking under a table while a tutor explained that an audience was always part of the work. At the time she had interpreted that as an artistic principle. Now she thought it might also have been advice about having chairs ready. Tom believed the exhibition would expose his inexperience, although nobody had criticised his work. Leila recognised the expression on his face because she had once mistaken being new for having committed an error.

Bev suspected the three spare screws belonged to an attachment, but had not seen that attachment. She made a little sketch instead of entering a lost-part claim. Leila approved the distinction. A piece without an obvious home was a question; it became a loss only when they knew what was absent. The empty label and the bell remained questions too. She collected them on a single page where they could wait without acquiring answers from the pressure of being inconvenient.

Tom intended to call the mayor after lunch, but had not dialled the number. Leila asked him to postpone it until the director checked the anonymous message. If the crate held a live performer, the bell might be a signal; nobody opened it on that assumption. Tom suggested this explanation with an expression which made it difficult to tell whether he was joking. Bev said that a performer with a bell should have insisted on a better contract. They left the straps alone and returned to measuring the pedestal.

By the end of the briefing, Tom had a corrected badge, a legible instruction sheet and a folder containing an intact delivery slip. Leila stayed in the exhibition workshop after the morning briefing. She closed her eyes on the bench while the kettle warmed. In a brief doze Leila dreamed that the pedestal delivered a speech, then woke to Tom’s real voice. He was asking where to put the empty cups. The pedestal had said nothing, but for the first time that morning Leila could answer a question without needing somebody in another building to return a call.
