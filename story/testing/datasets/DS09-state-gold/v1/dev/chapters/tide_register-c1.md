The harbour had supplied Lena with three different times for the same tide before she had finished her first cup of tea. The pilot's almanac gave one, the watch board another, and a boy selling folded charts outside the rope shop assured her that both were wrong. She bought the cheapest chart because its paper was dry, then stood beneath the awning until the boy found the change. After paying three coins for the copied chart, Lena had fifteen left. “There are fifteen coins left,” Lena said. It was a small professional expense, but she entered it in the purse book while she could still remember the seller's face. At Merrow Quay, officials had a habit of regarding missing receipts as evidence that the public had never paid. She had come to survey the northern channel, a job that required the town's tide register, and the register had vanished that morning.

Tomas opened the archive with his shoulder because the warped door caught against the first shelf. Inside, the air smelled of salt trapped in paper. He had placed towels beneath two windows and a bowl under a stain in the ceiling, making a household of the town's neglected measurements. Lena admired the arrangement until he asked whether she could complete the survey from a certified copy. She said a copy would be useful after she compared it with the original. He rubbed the pale skin beneath his spectacles. “The bridge is closed,” Tomas said. Through the far window she could see the inspector moving towards it. Rook crossed to the survey bridge and stayed beside its lever. The contradiction did not require an accusation. She asked Tomas what he meant by closed, and he said the public should not use it during the inspection. That was a narrower statement than the one he had first offered.

Lena had borrowed the brass key from the harbour clerk to reach the upper chart room. Tomas said she should not carry it into the rain, where the small ward could fill with grit. She placed the brass key in Tomas’s open hand. He tested the weight before putting it into a drawer that had no lock of its own. Lena knew Tomas now held the brass key. Outside, a figure passed behind the window at the end of the corridor. Ivo still believed Lena had the brass key, although he had watched the handover through the wrong window. The window reflected a mirror on the opposite wall, turning the order of their movements inside out. Lena noticed the watcher only as a dark coat receding towards the quay. Lena no longer trusted Tomas to interpret the archive without a witness. She asked him to leave the drawer open while she worked. He did so, but she could hear his objection in the care with which he moved the chair away from it.

Ivo arrived with a stack of blank forms and an explanation about the watch office needing a delivery signature. The pay register named Ivo Marr as a dock clerk. The clerk had copied that line for Lena earlier, adding a neat tick beside the wages figure. Ivo spoke as if his chief skill was carrying paper from one room to another without asking what it said. Lena gave him the empty forms she did not need and watched him avoid the tide shelf. Ivo carried the missing tide register under his coat. A corner of the leather binding appeared when he reached for his cap. She recognised the green stain the harbour chart had acquired from that same cover. He saw her recognise it and said he had been told to bring it back. She asked by whom. He answered by opening the book instead of naming the person.

The afternoon measurement occupied one page, with the following page already ruled for the next day. The register contained two entries for the same tide. At first Lena assumed the second line was a correction, but there was no crossed figure, explanatory mark, or initial beside either entry. The two observations claimed the same water and different fees. She turned the volume towards the window and examined the stitched edge. A blue thread protruded from the register’s stitched spine. Ivo tried to press it flat with his thumb. She asked him to leave it alone until they knew whether it belonged to the stitching. He said every old register had loose threads. That might be true, and it did not answer why this one lay across an opening in the leather. Tomas set three cups on the desk, making more noise than the crockery required.

Rook came back briefly to ask whether the missing volume had been found. He stood in the doorway rather than approach the open page. Rook wore a square impression of clean skin where his ring should have been. Lena had met him only once before, when he signed the temporary survey permit with a ring tapping the desk at every stroke. Now he folded that hand into his other sleeve. She asked whether the bridge lever was working. He said he would know when the test bell sounded and returned to the rain before she could ask about the second fee. Ivo shut the register. Lena did not stop him, because she had already copied both entries and wanted to know which person would object to her keeping the copy. Nobody did. The silence felt less like agreement than a room full of people waiting for another person to speak first.

They brought the survey marker from the porch before the weather worsened. Tomas fixed the red survey marker to the archive wall. The town used red for a measurement awaiting public verification, a convention Lena liked because it made uncertainty visible instead of disguising it as an incomplete shade of official blue. She reached above the shelf to steady the marker while Tomas drove the pin. The window shutter swung loose behind her. The falling shutter bruised Lena’s right wrist. Pain spread through the joint when she caught the wooden edge. Tomas wanted to send for the nurse at once. She said a bruise could wait until they had secured the shutter, which was still capable of striking someone else. Ivo held the hook while she stepped back. His hands knew the mechanism too readily for someone who claimed never to work in this room.

The rain thickened until the quay vanished behind the glass. Lena could not tell whether the wet footprints belonged to Rook. A person had crossed the corridor while the room was empty, leaving a track that ended at the register shelf. Ivo's boots were wet too. Tomas suggested the marks belonged to the nurse, though no nurse had arrived. Lena drew their outline on a scrap instead of selecting a convenient owner. She had learned that restraint during a winter job in another port, when a familiar boot size had nearly become a public charge. Lena remembered the winter when she had lived above the rope shop. The rope shop smelled warmer than this room, and the landlord had shouted at every customer as though the whole town owed him rent. She could not have named the year without finding an old letter. Remembering it did not tell her who had stood at the tide shelf today.

Tomas explained that the bridge bell gave the watch a signal when the channel was clear. Lena asked him to show the mechanism on the room's engineering plan. There was no automatic trigger, no water-driven chain, and no clock connection: the bell rang when a person moved the lever. He admitted that several official notices had described it as a tide signal without making that distinction. Ivo said everyone understood. Lena asked whether everyone included visitors navigating a narrow channel in fog. If the fog lifted, the northern buoy might become visible. Until then a captain could hear a bell and mistake a person's instruction for a measurement of the sea. Tomorrow, Lena intended to search every boat in the harbour. The search had not happened, and she wrote it under tomorrow rather than describe the boats as cleared. Ivo watched her divide the notebook page with a ruler and seemed, for the first time, interested in what she might discover.

They ate bread beside the copy stand while the rain beat at the shutter. Tomas talked about a hearing proposed for the next morning, as if saying the word public might excuse the room's smaller concealments. Tomas believed a public hearing would be safer than a private bargain. Lena asked him to bring the original register and the watch log. He said he would ask permission. She answered that records kept for the town ought to be available to the town's authorised surveyor. Ivo stared at the bread. He was still holding the register, but now he held it where she could see it. The blue thread had caught on his cuff. She wanted to pull it free, then decided that leaving one small thing undisturbed might be more useful than winning another argument before the evidence had arrived.

Lena remained in the archive when the public doors were locked. Tomas brought a blanket and a lamp whose wick he trimmed too short. The small flame made the copied figures look paler than they were. Lena placed a sheet over them to keep the damp from lifting the ink and listened for the bridge bell. It did not sound. She fell asleep at the copy stand with her wrist resting on the folded blanket. That night she dreamed the tide bell was ringing beneath the sea. In sleep the sound arrived through black water, and the two entries opened like doors beneath it. She woke before dawn to the scratch of someone moving a chair in the corridor. The lamp had gone out. Her purse book, the damp chart, and both copies remained where she had put them. She waited without calling, because the next sound might tell her whether the visitor knew she was awake. Lena settled at the archive desk to wait for morning.
