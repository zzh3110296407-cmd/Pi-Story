The taxi arrived while the first batch of rolls was still in the oven. Ruth insisted that Daniel turn them out before anyone helped her into the car. Her discomfort had started as a pressure beneath the ribs, something she described as an argument with yesterday's soup, but the colour had gone from her face when she tried to lift the cooling rack. Naomi called the clinic. She repeated the symptoms without naming a diagnosis, grateful to have a task that could be done correctly through attention alone. Daniel stood beside the oven wearing one glove and holding the other as if he had forgotten that it belonged to him.

Ruth handed the brass key to Daniel before the taxi left. She told him which bolt stuck and where the flour delivery receipt would be. He nodded after each instruction. Naomi saw how quickly an emergency could make him seem younger, not because he failed to act, but because he waited for their mother to approve every ordinary action. Ruth refused a coat and then asked for one after sitting in the car. They all pretended this was a practical adjustment rather than a measure of her fear. At the corner she asked whether the rolls were out. Naomi said they were, trusting Daniel with the fact without demanding another confirmation.

Naomi waited with Ruth in the neighbourhood clinic. The waiting room had a poster about winter appointments, a stack of magazines with carefully removed address labels, and a clock whose minute hand moved with a small audible effort. Ruth looked at the door every time it opened. Naomi asked whether she should call the landlord. Ruth said there was nothing the landlord could do about a stomach. That did not answer the question Naomi had intended, but it answered the one she had actually asked. She folded her phone into her hands and tried to learn the difference. Outside, a delivery bicycle rattled against a railing, and she knew Daniel had come before she saw him.

Daniel showed them the minor burn on his left wrist. He had caught it on the oven edge while moving a tray, and he seemed more annoyed by the need to explain it than by the injury itself. Ruth examined the mark, then remembered where they were and laughed once, without much humour. The nurse gave him something to cover it and asked which of them had the appointment. Daniel pointed at Ruth with the embarrassed relief of a boy who had avoided being examined. Naomi noticed flour in the folds of his helmet strap. He had closed the bakery, returned to check it, and then cycled hard enough to arrive breathing as if he had something important to say.

Daniel gave the letter back to Naomi outside the clinic. The street was too noisy for the conversation either of them had rehearsed. He said he had found another paper with it. Naomi asked whether he meant a sale agreement. His expression changed before he answered, and she realised that he had been preparing to defend something entirely different. He unfolded a certificate from a stiff brown sleeve. Daniel’s certificate established that he was a qualified piano tuner. The type was formal, the date recent enough to surprise her, and his name was printed with the middle initial he had stopped using after school. She read it twice while he watched the traffic.

“I never wanted anything to do with the piano,” Daniel said. He had said it often enough for it to sound like family history. Naomi did not contradict him immediately. The paper between them was better than a contradiction; it was evidence of work she had not known to ask about. She asked how long the course had taken. He told her, and then told her about the evening trains, the borrowed practice instrument, and the embarrassment of returning to study with people who assumed he was older and more settled than he felt. His denial remained something he had said, not a reliable account of everything he wanted.

Inside, Ruth had been taken to an observation room. Daniel went to tell her he would cover the evening deliveries. Naomi stayed outside long enough to read the receipt again. The unsigned receipt matched a payment in Ruth’s old union ledger. She had photographed that ledger the previous morning while looking for the window repair entry. The matching sum did not prove that the whole household fund had been spent secretly, nor that the bakery was in danger. It showed only that this payment had a recorded source. She wrote down the page number instead of the accusation she had been carrying since Mrs. Ames brought her soup.

Mrs. Ames called to say she had collected the blue tin at Ruth's request. Mrs. Ames took the blue tin to the community hall for safekeeping. The hall office had a locked cupboard, and Ruth disliked leaving important papers in an empty shop. This was a sensible explanation, offered by the same neighbour whose earlier explanation had sent Naomi searching for a buyer. Mrs. Ames believed the refund was a buyer’s deposit, but the repair shop’s receipt named it a returned overpayment. Naomi asked her to read the heading aloud before they discussed what it meant. There was a pause, then a small apologetic noise. A forty-pound refund brought the household fund to two hundred and sixty pounds. “The fund is two hundred and sixty pounds,” Naomi said.

Naomi recalled arriving at the upstairs apartment on the first evening of this visit. It had been only a day earlier, not the distant homecoming her tired mind was already making of it. She remembered the sheet over the piano and Daniel testing the delayed note. Daniel tapped the delayed piano key without explaining it. The memory did not move the piano into the clinic or show what he was doing now. It gave a different meaning to something she had seen at the time and dismissed as an old habit. She began to wonder how many of his habits were pieces of work he had never expected her to notice.

Naomi suspected her brother had hidden his qualification to avoid another family argument. Suspicion was easier than asking, but she was beginning to distrust its convenience. When Daniel returned to the waiting room, she told him that she had thought he wanted the bakery sold. He said he had thought she wanted that. For several minutes they compared memories of a telephone conversation neither had recorded. Each had heard an impatience that the other insisted was exhaustion. They could not recover the exact words. They could agree that both had made plans around an assumption and then treated the other's silence as confirmation. It was an uncomfortable agreement, but it was at least shared.

The clinic doctor said Ruth would remain for observation. Ruth immediately began dividing the next morning's tasks between them. Naomi asked for the lease register, expecting resistance. Ruth looked surprised, then told her it was in the blue tin, beneath the union papers. Naomi called Mrs. Ames again and asked her to check only the signature page. The answer came back plainly. Naomi knew the lease register still lacked both heirs’ signatures. Naomi and Daniel agreed to check the lease together before either answered the landlord. The bakery had not passed quietly into someone else's hands through an act that neither child could remember. There were other questions to ask, but that one could stop spreading through the house.

A pencilled date on the certificate matched their father’s last birthday. Daniel said Ruth had written it there because she remembered dates that way. Naomi saw him trying to decide whether to tell her more. She did not hurry him. He said their father had once offered to pay for a first course, and he had refused because an offer made during an argument had seemed like another way of choosing his life for him. Naomi asked whether the unsigned receipt was connected. Daniel said he did not know. It was the first answer either of them had given all day without trying to make it more useful than the evidence allowed.

Naomi intended to speak to the landlord the next morning. Before doing so she wanted to read the actual lease, not the family's account of what the lease might require. Daniel said the upstairs room could become a practice room if they moved the piano elsewhere. If they moved the piano, the room could hold a second table. Neither suggestion was an event that had already happened. They sketched possibilities on the back of an appointment card and left most of them uncrossed, an unfamiliar form of planning in a family that usually mistook deciding quickly for taking care of one another. Ruth listened from the observation-room doorway and asked whether anyone had fed the starter.

Ruth remained in the clinic’s observation room that evening. Daniel cycled back to attend to the dough, carrying the brass key, while Naomi stayed with the letter folded in her bag. The corridors grew quieter, though the waiting-room clock continued its patient struggle. She read the letter's first line, stopped, and read it again without looking ahead for an explanation of the receipt. Their father had begun by apologising for being better at practical gifts than at saying what they were for. Naomi found this irritatingly accurate. She also found that accuracy was not the same as an excuse, and that she could allow it to be the first without granting the second. Naomi believed Mrs. Ames would attend the hall meeting.

In the clinic chair she dreamed the bakery had no upstairs floor. She could hear the piano but could not find a staircase, and Daniel was waiting outside with a certificate that turned to flour whenever she tried to read it. A nurse woke her by asking whether she wanted tea. Naomi said yes and sat upright until the dream lost its authority over the room. Ruth was sleeping behind a real door. Daniel had sent a photograph of the covered dough. The letter remained in her bag, and the blue tin was at the community hall. These small, checkable facts were enough to get her through the next hour without inventing the one after it. Naomi remained at the clinic while Ruth finished her observation stay.
